## Core Teaching

### VA Onboarding: Defining the Role
Your first VA will feel clunky because you're building the role as you go, not having someone step into a predefined position. Spend the first two to three weeks being intentional about: what does the VA do each day? What do you keep? When do they escalate to you?

**The $50 rule:** If a decision is under $50, the VA makes it without asking. If it's a policy question for the future, that goes into the weekly review — not a text at 4 PM that makes you feel like you need to fix it immediately.

**Weekly review cadence:** Set a standing call (e.g., Friday) where the VA brings: "Here's what I feel needs to be done. Here's the policies that need to change." That's when you discuss, not in real time throughout the day.

**Daily end-of-day SOP (checklist).** The host's version is in the Logistics course in Skool. It has about ten items the VA checks off before clocking out. Examples: called first-time cleans that finished today using the post-clean script (review ask, check satisfaction, try to convert to recurring), checked this week's and next week's cleans for double bookings, and other daily tasks. Take the host's version, customize it to your business, and throw away what doesn't fit.

**Daily debrief with new VA:** Set up a 15–30 minute end-of-day call — "How did the day go? What did you do? What questions do you have?" Rub shoulders constantly, especially in the virtual world. The more contact between important people, the faster the business grows. The host and Elijah are in offices next to each other for this reason.

**Write down what you hate but the business needs.** Lead sheet updates, rescheduling calls, double-booking checks — those are VA tasks. Don't carry the emotional burden of tasks that someone else can do for $5/hour.

### Cleaner Capacity Math
One cleaner can do two cleans per day (morning and afternoon — pretty standard). If the average ticket is $244 (the host's March average), that's $488/day. Working Monday through Friday, 20 days/month = ~$9,760/month per cleaner.

You can run a $20K/month business with two full-time cleaners and one of them working two weekends a month. The key is having backup availability — cleaners who work for other companies too but can pick up jobs when needed. The host's setup: four main cleaners (almost daily, usually two to three jobs/day), a subcontractor with multiple crews who took seven to eight cleans last month, and two additional backups.

Courtney is doing $17.2K/month with three and a half cleaners (one part-time). That's extremely efficient. Rick/Melissa have 10 available cleaners but most work only two to three days/week, so capacity per cleaner is lower.

### Carpet Cleaning: Subcontract or Own It
Courtney's mega-church client wants carpet cleaning. Two options:

**Option 1: Subcontract.** Find a carpet cleaning company in your area. Get two to three quotes. Walk them through the job. Be transparent: "I'm looking for a subcontractor I can make a little margin on." If they charge $200, you charge the church $250–$300. Critical: you are the point of contact — the church never gets the subcontractor's number. The church wants one person to talk to, and that's you.

**Option 2: Buy the machine.** A carpet cleaner rents for ~$40. Buying one is ~$300–$400. If a current cleaner's partner has carpet cleaning experience, pay them a one-time training fee to teach the cleaner. Then you own the machine, have higher margins, and can upsell carpet cleaning to other commercial clients. Once-a-month frequency means it's manageable. The host knows someone doing $30K/month in carpet cleaning alone alongside $20K/month in house cleaning — it's a real business on its own.

Once you have the capability, email/text your database: "We now offer professional carpet cleaning" — and watch people bite.

### Social Media Strategy
The approach mirrors Alex Hormozi's: for most cleaning businesses, social media will not be how you get clients. It increases the conversion rate of leads who already found you. A snoopy prospect finds your website, likes what they see, clicks to your Instagram, and sees: here's our team, how we got started, what we stand for, why we're different, before/afters, review screenshots. They're now more sold. The lead didn't come from social media, but social media increased their confidence.

What to post: about us, how you got started, your team, before/afters, satisfaction guarantee, review screenshots, "why choose us" content. Frequency: once a week minimum to check the box of "we're legit, we still exist, we still clean." The host's VA posts about five times a week but he literally hadn't looked at it in months — it runs in the background.

What NOT to do: don't expect social media to be a lead source. Don't spend time on it at the expense of paid marketing or GBP optimization. "I don't care to do that. I want to be hands off. Do I recommend it? Yeah, sure. I don't want to do it because I want to be hands off." Posting before/afters with 22 followers — nobody's finding those posts. It's just a credibility check for people who already know about you.

Connecting social media to your GBP does benefit your search presence slightly.

### Spanish-Speaking Cleaners and Labor Cost Reduction
Labor is typically 50% of revenue. Spanish-speaking cleaners can bring that to 30–35% because labor rates are lower ($12–$22/hour vs. $28–$50+ for English-speaking cleaners). Two approaches: (1) Learn Spanish yourself (one owner, Salim in Canada at $90K/month, learned Spanish from scratch over 18 months). (2) Get a Spanish-speaking VA from South America (slightly more expensive than Philippines) who can translate between clients and cleaners by phone.

The host's subcontractor Oscar speaks Spanish and English — when there's a communication issue between a Spanish-speaking cleaner and a client, Oscar gets a call and translates in real time. The host has considered switching to this model to cut labor costs, but hasn't yet. It's presented as something to think about for margin-constrained businesses.

### Database Blast Results
Courtney went through four to five pages of old contacts in Harvest CRM, sending reactivation messages. Result so far: five bookings, plus clean-up of the database (people who moved, not interested — removed). About 50 contacts left to send to. This validates the "Old Leads Are Not Dead Leads" principle — blasting old leads before turning ads back on is a real revenue source.

### Labor Bottleneck Timing
When you're fully booked and turning away clients, pause marketing (or at least reduce spend) until the labor bottleneck is solved. Use the downtime to focus on VA setup, SOPs, and systems — prepare for scale before it arrives. Once new cleaners are test-cleaned and proven, ramp marketing back up. The host experienced this firsthand: scaling without a VA meant waking at 6:30 AM, working from bed until 9 AM, and being on the phone fixing things all day. "We cannot grow like this."

## Step-by-Step Process

### Setting Up the VA Role (First 2–3 Weeks)
1. Define the VA's daily tasks (what they own vs. what you keep).
2. Create or customize a daily end-of-day checklist (start from the host's version in the Logistics course).
3. Set the $50 decision rule — under $50, the VA decides without asking.
4. Establish a weekly review call where the VA brings policy questions and process suggestions.
5. Do a daily 15–30 minute debrief with the new VA at end of day.
6. Write down everything you hate doing that the business needs — those are VA tasks.
7. Set escalation protocols: when does the VA text you vs. wait for the weekly review?

### Subcontracting a Service You Don't Offer
1. Identify the need (e.g., carpet cleaning for a commercial client).
2. Find 2–3 potential subcontractors in your area. Call them.
3. Walk them through the job. Be transparent: "I'm looking for a subcontractor I can make margin on."
4. Get quotes. Your price to the client is their quote plus your margin.
5. You are the point of contact — the client never gets the subcontractor's info.
6. If the subcontractor needs to communicate with the client directly, they identify as being with your company.
7. Alternative: buy the equipment ($300–$400), have a current cleaner trained, and keep higher margins.

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Not defining the VA role before onboarding — it will be clunky but you need to be intentional.
- Letting the VA text you policy questions in real time instead of saving them for a weekly review.
- Not doing daily debriefs with a new VA — the virtual world makes it easy to disconnect.
- Continuing to spend on marketing when you're booked solid and turning clients away — pause and use the time for systems.
- Scaling without a VA — you'll burn out working from bed at 6:30 AM.
- Giving a subcontractor's contact info to your client — you lose the relationship.
- Expecting social media to generate leads instead of treating it as a credibility check.
- Posting before/afters to 22 followers and expecting results — nobody's finding those posts.
- Not putting a phone number on GBP posts — Google will flag it; use the "Call Now" button.
- Hiring automation not triggering because a new form wasn't connected to the pipeline automation.
- Harvest CRM emails going to spam — likely because the native email isn't connected; use your actual business email.
- Not looking at your numbers — Courtney didn't realize she was at $17.2K until prompted on the call.
- Treating cleaners as "full-time employees" on your website when they're subcontractors — Texas Workforce Commission and IRS issues.
