## Core Teaching

### Building Connection in the First Two Minutes
The first two minutes of a call are a huge opportunity for connection that most people waste. When you need a moment to pull up your screen (or even if you don't), use loaded questions to build rapport:

"How's your day going?" is fine but shallow. Better: ask where they're located, then "How long have you been around there?" This opens up a much deeper conversation. If they've been there a while: "So you've seen a lot of change in the area, huh?" (they get passionate about how different things are). If they're newer: "Oh, so you're kind of new around here. What made you move?" These questions make the prospect feel like a person, not a transaction.

Elijah uses "How long have you been around?" on almost every call. It naturally leads into personal territory that builds trust.

Even if your screen is already pulled up, delay a few seconds: "Give me one second here. Let me pull this up. How's your day going?" You're not wasting time — you're building the connection that makes the sale easier.

### Give Context Before the Price (The Doctor Analogy)
Never drop a price without context. Think of it like a doctor visit: if you tell a doctor your back hurts and they immediately say "$300 for Vicodin, want it?" — that feels transactional. Instead, a good doctor mirrors your symptoms back ("so you're having trouble bending over, can't work out"), describes the solution and why it works ("this works 99% of the time, it's the best thing doctors prescribe"), and then gives the price.

Before giving the price on a cleaning call: recap what they need ("so it's a 4-bed, 2.5-bath move-out, 1800 sqft"), share what makes you different ("we have a 100% satisfaction guarantee, five stars, all cleaners background-checked"), and frame the solution as specifically built for them. Then give the price. The price feels justified because it sits inside a context of value.

### Handling "I'm Comparing Quotes" — Ask More Questions
When someone says "I'm getting other quotes" or "I'll call you back," don't end the conversation. Ask questions:

"Absolutely. When you're comparing, what specifically are you looking for — best price, best quality, or both?" This tells you what lever to pull.

If they say price: "Okay, you don't want to go with the cheapest and then have to clean up after them, right? We're a bit more expensive, but we have a satisfaction guarantee. And we can actually create a custom plan that's more affordable while still getting the job done."

If they say quality: "That's actually why most of our best customers call around — they check others and end up coming back to us because of [X]."

The core principle: keep asking questions until you understand what they actually need. The only way to give someone a solution that feels like "exactly what I'm looking for" is to deeply understand what they're looking for.

### The Custom Solution Technique (For Price-Sensitive Leads)
When someone has a budget problem, don't just lower the price — restructure the service:

"Something we do for people is we handle the heavy lifting — bathrooms, appliances, the harder stuff. And if you want to take care of the vacuuming and the easier parts, we have a more affordable option. Would that make sense for you?"

This reframes the conversation from "your price is too high" to "let's build exactly what you need." People who were going to do it themselves ($0) suddenly have a $150–$200 option that takes the hardest work off their plate.

Rick's win: on a live call, the prospect wanted bi-weekly cleaning for a big house. Instead of quoting the full price, Rick said "your price is going to seem high for bi-weekly — do you need every room every time?" She said no. He created a custom plan: deep clean first, then alternating bi-weekly — one week main floor only, next week whole house. She said: "This is exactly what I was looking for." Booked on the first call.

### Never Be the One to End the Conversation (Elijah's Rule)
Elijah's door-to-door sales principle: never be the one to end the conversation. Keep it going until the prospect either books or firmly says no. Even when they push back ("we'll take care of it"), ask: "How do you plan to take care of it?" or "What made you think that option was best?" — and suddenly they're back in the conversation.

This feels unnatural at first, especially if you have high emotional intelligence — you sense the pushback and want to stop. But the skill of sales is being able to feel that pushback and instead of stopping, ask another question. You're not selling — you're trying to understand.

On the phone, the equivalent: when they say "I'll call you back," don't say "sounds good, just let me know." Instead: "Great, I have your email here — I'll shoot you this info. What specifically are you looking for when you compare? Best price, best quality?" You're back in the conversation.

### Follow-Up Cadence for Non-Closers
When someone doesn't book on the call: call the next day, then two days later, then text. Keep following up until they give a yes or no. Until they say no, you're not bothering them — they reached out to you. If you truly feel like you're bugging someone: "I don't want to bug you. I don't mean to. I just wanted to see if it would work. Wish you the best."

## Step-by-Step Process

### Handling "I'm Comparing Quotes" on a Call
1. Don't panic or end the conversation. Respond: "Absolutely, I totally get that."
2. Ask: "When you're comparing, what are you looking for — best price, best quality, or both?"
3. Listen to their answer — it tells you the real objection.
4. If price: "We can create a custom plan that's more affordable while still getting the job done. What areas are most important to you?"
5. If quality: "Our best customers call around and come back to us because of [satisfaction guarantee / reviews / vetting]. I love that you care about your home."
6. Build the custom solution together: "We handle the heavy stuff — bathrooms, appliances. You take care of the easier parts. Does that sound like what you're looking for?"
7. Get their email: "I'll shoot this info over to you — what's your email? What do you want to see in that email?"
8. You're now back in the sale. Continue until they book or firmly decline.

### Pre-Price Context Building
1. Recap their needs: "So we're looking at a 4-bed, 2.5-bath, 1800 sqft move-out."
2. Share differentiators: "We have a 100% satisfaction guarantee. Five stars. All cleaners background-checked, insured, and bring their own supplies."
3. Frame the solution: "Based on what you're telling me, here's what I'd recommend..."
4. Then give the price: "That would be about $330."
5. If resistance: "I hear you. Let me see what we can do to make this work for you."

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Giving the price without any context or value-building first.
- Ending the conversation when the prospect says "I'll call you back" or "I'm comparing."
- Taking pushback as a signal to stop instead of a signal to ask more questions.
- Not offering a custom/flexible plan for price-sensitive leads — just quoting the full price and losing them.
- Wasting the first two minutes on silence or shallow small talk.
- Telling the prospect everything about your company in a monologue instead of asking questions.
- Closing with a statement ("I'll send it over") instead of a question ("what are you looking for in that email?").
- Not following up aggressively enough — calling once and giving up.
- Male VAs in cleaning tend to close at lower rates than female VAs — gender matters in this industry.
