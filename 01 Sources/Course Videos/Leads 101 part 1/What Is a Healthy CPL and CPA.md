# What Is a Healthy CPL and CPA (understanding healthy lead and acquisition costs in a cleaning business)

## Best For
Cleaning business owners who are running paid marketing and need to understand whether their cost per lead, cost per acquisition, and first-clean profitability make sense.
## Problems This Solves
- Not knowing whether a cost per lead is good or bad.
- Focusing too much on CPL instead of cost per acquisition.
- Panicking when the first clean is not immediately profitable.
- Spending on Meta, PPC, or LSA without understanding close rate and booking economics.
- Comparing marketing platforms without accounting for lead quality.
## Quick Summary
This lesson explains that there is no single universal healthy CPL or CPA because every area and platform is different. From experience, LSA can become difficult when the cost is over $50 per call, especially if the lead quality is low. Meta can work at a $5 to $10 cost per lead, but $15 to $25 per lead can be rough because the lead quality is often lower. PPC can still make sense at $30 to $50 per lead if the quality and close rate are strong. The bigger point is that cost per acquisition matters more than cost per lead. Ideally, the first clean should make money, but it can be normal to lose a little if recurring clients make up the difference over time. The numbers should make sense over a two- to three-month period.
## Core Teaching
The main teaching is that CPL by itself does not tell the whole story. A cheap lead can still be bad if it does not close, and an expensive lead can still be profitable if it turns into a good customer.

A healthy cost per acquisition means the numbers make sense when a lead becomes a booking. The ideal situation is to make money on the first clean. However, it can be normal to lose a little on the first clean if enough customers become recurring and stay long enough to make the money back. The warning is that you should not be losing a lot on the first clean.

The lesson gives platform-specific context from experience. For LSA, paying more than $50 per call can become difficult, especially if the area brings in a lot of price shoppers. For Meta, $5 to $10 cost per lead can be okay, while $15 to $25 can be rough because Meta lead quality is often lower. For PPC, anything under $40 cost per lead can be good if quality and close rate are strong, and even $30 to $50 per lead can work if cost per acquisition is healthy.

The way to judge marketing is to work backward. Look at cost per lead, close rate, cost per acquisition, average ticket, and whether customers stay recurring. CPL matters, but CPA matters more because CPA tells you whether the marketing is actually producing profitable bookings.
## Key Principles
- There is no exact healthy CPL or CPA for every market.
- Cost per acquisition matters more than cost per lead.
- Lead quality and close rate change whether a CPL is healthy.
- The ideal is to make money on the first clean.
- It can be acceptable to lose a little on the first clean if recurring revenue makes it back.
- You should not lose a lot of money on the first clean.
- Marketing should be judged over a two- to three-month period, not one isolated lead.
- Different platforms have different healthy CPL ranges.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Identify the platform the lead is coming from: LSA, Meta, PPC, or another channel.
2. Calculate your cost per lead for that platform.
3. Calculate your close rate for that platform.
4. Use those numbers to understand your cost per acquisition or cost per booking.
5. Compare the acquisition cost to your first-clean profit or loss.
6. Look at how many customers stay recurring and whether the money is made back over two to three months.
7. Optimize the ads or sales process if CPL, close rate, or CPA does not make sense.

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Trying to use one universal CPL target for every area.
- Judging a channel only by cost per lead instead of cost per acquisition.
- Ignoring close rate and lead quality.
- Assuming a first-clean loss is always bad without looking at recurring retention.
- Accepting a large first-clean loss without proving the backend makes it profitable.
