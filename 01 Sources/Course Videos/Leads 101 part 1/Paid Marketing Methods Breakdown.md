This lesson breaks down the best lead engines for a cleaning business based on what was actually tested and what was working at the time of the lesson. The main point is that you do not “try marketing”; you build a lead engine you can predict. That means tracking cost per lead, close rate, and cost per booking for every lead source. PPC, Thumbtack, and Google Business Profile are presented as the strongest options, with Google Business Profile being the best-performing source in this example. Core Teaching
The big idea is simple: a cleaning business should not be built on “posting and hoping.” It should be built on lead engines you can measure. Every lead should be tracked so you know where it came from, what it cost, whether it booked, and what the cost per booking was.
PPC, specifically Google Ads, is strong because it captures people who are already looking for a cleaner. In the lesson’s example, PPC averaged $43 cost per lead with a 41% close rate, which came out to about $104 cost per booking. The warning is that PPC has to be set up properly; otherwise, you can spend thousands and have almost nothing to show for it.
Thumbtack is presented as a cheap booking source when it is run correctly. The lesson’s numbers were about $15 per lead, a 26% close rate, and roughly $58 cost per booking. The key is setting it up to target people looking for recurring service, not just one-time deep cleans.
Local Map Pack SEO through Google Business Profile is described as the best lead source in the lesson. In Q4, the business got 115 leads and booked 67 of them from this source. It is “free” in the sense that you do not pay per lead, but the lesson notes that getting the best results usually requires a Google business address, website optimization, and consistent GBP posting.
Facebook Ads were tested and failed for the business at that time: more than $1,000 spent for only 2 bookings. Yelp is called out as a channel to avoid because of fees and poor feedback from other cleaning business owners. LSA had not been used because calls were expensive in the area and appeared to bring more price shoppers.
The hidden lead engine most people ignore is past clients and old leads. The lesson stresses that your CRM should send long-term follow-up over 2 months, 6 months, and even 12 months because old leads and one-time clean clients can turn into bookings later.

Step-by-Step Process
Track every lead in a tracking sheet or CRM.
For each lead source, calculate cost per lead, close rate, and cost per booking.
Start with the highest-ROI lead engines first, such as PPC, Thumbtack, and Google Business Profile when they fit your area.
Set up PPC properly so you are not wasting money on poor campaign structure.
Configure Thumbtack to target people looking for recurring service.
Optimize your website and consistently post on your Google Business Profile.
Avoid channels that have already shown poor results, like Yelp in this lesson.
Build long-term CRM follow-up for old leads and past clients.
Add a more intentional referral system.
Reinvest into the channels with the best numbers before testing lower-ROI channels.

Cleaned Lesson Notes
Here are the best lead engines for a cleaning business based on what we have actually tested and what is working right now.
The big idea is that you do not “try marketing.” You build a lead engine you can predict. That means you track cost per lead, close rate, and cost per booking. Use the free tracking sheet in the Free Resources module. Every lead should go into that sheet so you know your KPIs.
Our Q4 2025 numbers were:
PPC, specifically Google Ads, averaged $43 cost per lead with a 41% close rate. That works out to about $104 cost per booking. PPC is strong because it captures intent. People are actively searching for a cleaner, and once it is dialed in, it gives you consistency and scale.
Thumbtack was about $15 cost per lead when factoring in the free leads hack shown in the Free Resources module. Our close rate was 26%, which means roughly $58 cost per booking. Thumbtack is especially good when you set it up to target people looking for recurring service, not just one-time deep cleans.
Google Business Profile and Local Map Pack SEO have been our best source. In Q4, we got 115 leads and booked 67 of them. GBP is “free,” but getting the best results usually requires a Google business address. Depending on your city, that can run $50–$200 per month. We pay $80 per location and have two locations. It has been amazing, but it is not magic. You still have to optimize your website and consistently post on your GBP.
Facebook Ads failed for us at the time of the lesson. We spent more than $1,000 for only 2 bookings. We probably did it wrong, so we were retesting with friends who were getting under $100 cost per booking.
Yelp is something I would avoid. We tested it and it was terrible. The fees were sneaky, and the “free trial” is something to watch out for. I have not personally heard one cleaning business owner say anything consistently good about Yelp.
Past clients and old leads are the hidden lead engine most people ignore. This is why your CRM matters. Your CRM should automatically send long-term follow-up at 2 months, 6 months, and even 12 months. We regularly get bookings from one-time clean clients from months ago and old leads who reply 3–6 months later to an automated follow-up text.
Referrals can be one of the best engines, but we have not fully tapped into it yet. We are working on improving our referral focus and building a more intentional system around it.
We have not used LSA because in our area it is often $60+ per call, and from what others report, it can bring a lot of price shoppers. Once we have more marketing budget, we may test lower-ROI methods like this, but it has not been our focus.
Rule of thumb: a healthy cleaning business usually needs to spend at least 10% of gross revenue on marketing if you want consistent growth. You cannot build stability on “posting and hoping.” You build it with lead engines you can measure.
