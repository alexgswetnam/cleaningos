# Red Flags to Avoid with Cleaners (spotting cleaners who are likely to create problems before or shortly after onboarding)

## Best For
Cleaning business owners interviewing, onboarding, or deciding whether to keep a cleaner who is already showing problems.
## Problems This Solves
- Moving forward with cleaners who do not feel like a good fit.
- Ignoring lateness or repeated rescheduling.
- Confusing janitorial or personal cleaning experience with professional house cleaning.
- Keeping problematic cleaners because of emotion.
- Letting emotions run hiring and firing decisions.
## Quick Summary
This lesson lists early cleaner red flags that should make an owner slow down or stop moving forward. If someone does not feel like a good fit in the interview, the recommendation is not to move forward. Lateness, repeated rescheduling, poor English, and lack of real house cleaning experience are all warning signs. The lesson makes a clear distinction between professional home cleaning and janitorial, office, or personal house cleaning experience. It also warns that if someone is already causing issues during the first week, it probably will not get better. The bigger message is to make logical business decisions instead of keeping a cleaner emotionally when the business evidence says it is not working.
## Core Teaching
The core teaching is that red flags usually do not fix themselves. If a cleaner is late, reschedules repeatedly, lacks the right experience, or is already difficult early on, the owner should not ignore that information.

A cleaner needs real house cleaning experience. Cleaning an office, doing janitorial work, or cleaning their own house is not the same as cleaning a client’s home professionally. If the business is built around high-quality residential cleaning, the cleaner needs experience in that specific environment.

The lesson also points to the owner’s emotions as a red flag. Some owners know a cleaner is not good for the business but still hesitate because they feel bad. The teaching is that business decisions need to be logical and based on what is best for the business, while still treating people well.
## Key Principles
- If the interview feels wrong, do not force it.
- Repeated rescheduling is a major warning sign.
- Professional house cleaning experience matters.
- Personal cleaning experience does not count the same way.
- Problems in week one usually do not magically improve.
- Emotions should not run hiring and firing decisions.
- Treat people well, but make business calls logically.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Evaluate fit during the interview.
2. Watch for lateness or rescheduling before onboarding.
3. Confirm they have actual residential cleaning experience.
4. Do not count “I clean my own house” as professional experience.
5. After onboarding, watch the first week carefully for problems.
6. Use a three-strike style system if appropriate, but move on quickly when the pattern is obvious.
7. Choose the business decision over the emotional decision.
## Scripts, Examples, or Phrases to Keep
- “Immediate, I don’t think this is going to be good.”
- “Rescheduling once is kind of okay, second time is like, yeah, this isn’t a good fit.”
- “Cleaning a home is very different.”
- “If someone says, I’ve got experience because I’ve cleaned around my own house, no, that doesn’t count.”
- “Your emotions shouldn’t be what’s running the business.”
## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Ignoring the gut feeling that someone is not a fit.
- Letting a cleaner reschedule over and over.
- Hiring someone with only office, janitorial, or personal cleaning experience.
- Trying to stick with a cleaner emotionally when it does not make sense.
- Assuming early problems will improve without evidence.
