## Problems This Solves
- Paying slower cleaners more money for dragging out a job.
- Penalizing the best cleaners because they finish faster.
- Creating a pricing model that pushes away great cleaners.
- Blurring the line between 1099 contractor and employee.
- Not understanding why bigger cleaning businesses usually avoid hourly contractor pay.
## Quick Summary
This lesson argues that paying cleaners hourly is dangerous for both business and legal reasons. Hourly pay tells cleaners that taking longer means making more, which can slow down jobs and make the client experience worse. It also penalizes the best cleaners because a cleaner who completes a hard four-hour job in three hours gets paid less for being efficient. The lesson says this model can push away great cleaners and is not ideal when small or at scale. It also warns that hourly pay can make the 1099 versus employee line more risky unless documentation and invoicing are extremely tight. The preferred direction is fixed job pricing, where cleaners are paid for the completed job outcome rather than clocking in and out.
## Core Teaching
The main teaching is that hourly pay creates the wrong incentives. If a cleaner knows that longer work equals more pay, the cleaner has a natural reason to stretch the job. The client gets a longer, slower clean, and the business pays more for that slower clean.

The lesson also points out the opposite problem: great cleaners get punished. If a strong cleaner finishes a hard job in less time while still doing it well, hourly pay means they earn less for being better. That is not the kind of pricing model that attracts or keeps the best cleaners.

The lesson also raises a 1099 concern. Paying contractors like they are clocking in and out can start to look more like an employee arrangement unless the documentation and invoicing are handled very carefully. The business should look at how it treats contractors and whether the structure would hold up if audited.

The operational preference is fixed job price. Pay cleaners for the job and the outcome, not for stretching time.
## Key Principles
- Hourly pay rewards slowness.
- Hourly pay can penalize fast, high-quality cleaners.
- Fixed job pricing aligns pay with completing the job.
- Cleaner pay structure affects both quality and retention.
- 1099 contractor pay needs to be handled carefully.
- Bigger operators usually have a reason for avoiding hourly contractor pay.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Look at whether your current pay model rewards speed and quality or rewards taking longer.
2. Stop thinking only in hourly terms and start thinking in completed job outcomes.
3. Set a fixed job price that makes sense for the cleaner and the business.
4. Make sure documentation and invoicing language fits the contractor relationship you are using.
5. Audit how you treat cleaners so the business model is not accidentally operating like employment.
## Scripts, Examples, or Phrases to Keep

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Paying contractors to clock in and clock out like employees.
- Making slow work more profitable than efficient work.
- Thinking hourly pay is safer just because it feels simple.
- Ignoring the 1099 versus employee issue in your pay structure.
