# When to “Fire” a Client (deciding when a client no longer makes sense for the schedule, profit, or business)
## Module
When to _Fire_ a Client
## Course
Labor 101

Not provided.
## Best For
Cleaning business owners dealing with difficult, low-paying, far-away, or schedule-blocking clients.
## Problems This Solves
- Keeping clients who take up valuable schedule spots.
- Accepting low-paying jobs that block better jobs.
- Continuing with clients who are difficult, late to pay, or constantly rescheduling.
- Not knowing whether to raise the price or end the relationship.
- Feeling guilty about letting a client go.
## Quick Summary
This lesson explains that you fire a client when keeping them no longer makes sense. The decision depends on the stage of the business because smaller businesses may tolerate things they would not tolerate later. The speaker evaluates whether the client is taking a schedule spot that could make more money, require less drive time, create fewer conversations, and be easier to serve. Examples include a low-paying biweekly client who only wanted a specific time and blocked bigger jobs, and a weekly client who paid late through Zelle, skipped weeks, and was disliked by the cleaner. The lesson recommends giving the client the option to continue at a price that actually makes sense. If they do not accept, wish them well and move on.
## Core Teaching
The core teaching is that not every client is worth keeping. A client may be technically paying you, but still costing the business through schedule disruption, low price, long drive time, late payments, cleaner frustration, or constant conversations.

The key question is: would this spot on the schedule be better used by another client who pays more, creates fewer problems, and makes the cleaner’s route easier? If the answer is yes, then keeping the current client may be costing the business money and time.

The lesson does not say to immediately drop every inconvenient client. Instead, determine what price or terms would make the client worth keeping. Then offer that option honestly. If the client accepts, great. If not, apologize, explain that it no longer works for the business, and wish them the best.

This becomes more important as the business grows. Early on, you may take jobs you would not take later. But over time, the schedule should be protected for clients that actually make sense.
## Key Principles
- Fire a client when keeping them no longer makes sense.
- A schedule spot has opportunity cost.
- Low pay, long drive, strict timing, and difficult communication all matter.
- The cleaner’s experience matters too.
- Sometimes the answer is a price increase, not immediate cancellation.
- Let the client choose whether the new price or terms work.
- Bigger businesses should be more selective than tiny businesses.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Identify what the client is costing in schedule space, drive time, conversations, late payments, and cleaner frustration.
2. Ask whether a better client could fill that spot.
3. Decide what price or terms would make the client worth keeping.
4. Communicate the change calmly and professionally.
5. Give the client the option to continue at the new price or terms.
6. If they decline, end the relationship kindly and move on.
7. Use the freed schedule spot for a better-fit client.
## Scripts, Examples, or Phrases to Keep
- “We’re not able to continue with our route and our paths and the distance unless it’s this amount.”
- “It only would make sense for us to continue this cleaning if it was $150 a cleaning.”
- “I totally understand that this might be a frustration for you.”
- “I apologize. I wish you the best and I hope you find a great cleaner that works out for you.”
- “I would only do it at this price.”
## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Keeping a client just because they are already on the schedule.
- Ignoring late payments and constant reschedules.
- Letting a low-paying job block a higher-value job.
- Not factoring in cleaner drive time or frustration.
- Firing emotionally instead of making a business decision.
- Failing to offer a price that would make it worth it.
