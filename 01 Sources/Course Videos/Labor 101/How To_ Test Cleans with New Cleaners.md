# How to Test Cleans with New Cleaners (using paid test cleans to evaluate cleaner fit, quality, communication, and client experience)

## Problems This Solves
- Cleaners sounding good in interviews but failing in homes.
- Not knowing what to look for during a test clean.
- Sending new cleaners alone to difficult clients or hard cleans.
- Skipping client and established-cleaner feedback.

## Quick Summary
This lesson explains why test cleans are important when bringing on a new cleaner. A cleaner can look good on paper and interview well, but the business does not really know until they are inside a home. Test cleans should be paid, but the cleaner should understand that the business is doing a slow on-ramp and evaluating fit. The speaker recommends pairing the new cleaner with an established cleaner, checking the client’s feedback, and watching for professionalism, communication, speed, quality, and smell or presentation issues. Test cleans should start with smaller, easier jobs, not move-outs, first-time deep cleans, problematic clients, or extreme clutter. The lesson also explains that cleaners should check each other’s work, walk through with the client at the end, and be evaluated by outcomes on the checklist.
## Core Teaching
The main teaching is that a test clean reveals what an interview cannot. Someone may seem experienced and nice, but the business needs to see how they act in a real client home.

The test clean is paid, but it should be framed as a slow on-ramp. The cleaner is not being promised long-term work immediately. The business is asking: are we a good fit for each other?

The preferred setup is to pair the test cleaner with an established cleaner. The established cleaner can observe quality, communicate missed items, and check work before the client walkthrough. The business also checks with the client after the clean.

The test should not be a risky job. Do not start with a move-out clean, first-time deep clean, problematic client, extreme clutter, or a client who expects a specific regular cleaner. Start with a smaller job where a reclean is manageable if something goes wrong.

The evaluation includes professionalism, client communication, smell/presentation, speed, quality, details like corners and baseboards, cross-contamination, willingness to ask questions, and whether they follow outcome-based checklists. A new support cleaner may also be paid differently than an established lead cleaner.
## Key Principles
- Interviews are not enough; cleaners must be tested in a home.
- Test cleans should be paid.
- A test clean is a slow on-ramp, not a guaranteed long-term role.
- Pair new cleaners with established cleaners when possible.
- Start with easier, smaller jobs.
- Do not test on high-risk clients or hard clean types.
- Evaluate both cleaning quality and client-facing professionalism.
- Checklists should describe outcomes, not micromanaged methods.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Interview the cleaner first.
2. Send expectations, guidelines, recommendations, and checklist documents before the test.
3. Choose a smaller, lower-risk job for the test clean.
4. Pair the new cleaner with an established cleaner.
5. Have both cleaners check each other’s work before leaving.
6. Walk through the home with the client at the end.
7. Ask the established cleaner for feedback.
8. Check with the client after the clean.
9. Evaluate professionalism, speed, quality, communication, details, and outcome completion.
10. Decide whether to continue the slow on-ramp, assign easier work, or stop using the cleaner.
## Scripts, Examples, or Phrases to Keep
- “Until they’re actually in a home, you don’t really know if they’re a good fit.”
- “We’re going to do a slow on ramp and do some test cleans.”
- “Are we a good fit for each other?”
- “I wouldn’t be doing move outs, first-time deep cleans, any clients that seem problematic, extreme clutter, anything like that for a test clean.”
- “They should be talking to a client when they get there, walking through, understanding the home and doing a walkthrough at the end.”
## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Sending a new cleaner alone to a hard job.
- Testing on a move-out clean or problematic client.
- Not getting feedback from the client.
- Not pairing the new cleaner with an established cleaner.
- Ignoring professionalism issues like being on speakerphone or smelling bad.
- Only evaluating speed and ignoring quality or communication.
- Being too quick to fully trust a cleaner after one good interview.
