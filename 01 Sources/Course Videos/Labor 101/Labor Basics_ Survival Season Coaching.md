## Problems This Solves
- Not knowing what a test clean should look like.
- Being afraid a new cleaner will not meet the owner’s standards.
- Confusing outcome expectations with illegal training or control of a 1099 cleaner.
- Not knowing how to split pay between the business and cleaners.
- Paying a cleaner before the job is actually complete.
- Not knowing what to do when a cleaner misses work, does a poor clean, or a client complains.
- Trying to rely on one cleaner instead of building coverage and quality options.
## Quick Summary
This coaching lesson walks through the messy reality of moving from cleaning everything yourself into using subcontractor cleaners. The main idea is that a test clean is not a guarantee of long-term work; it is a way to see if the cleaner does a good job, communicates well, and can meet the required outcome. The lesson explains that with 1099 cleaners you can set the end result and recommend tools or methods, but you cannot force the exact process, supplies, or step-by-step technique. Pay is discussed as a business decision, with a general recommendation around splitting revenue between the business and cleaners while still making sure cleaners earn enough to accept the work. The lesson also covers what to do when a cleaner misses things, including sending them back to fix the job when they did not complete the promised outcome. A big theme is that not every cleaner should get every job; some cleaners are great for recurring standard cleans but not deep cleans or move-outs. The owner’s job is to test carefully, place cleaners where they fit, keep client expectations clear, and make business decisions without being ruled by fear.
## Core Teaching
The core teaching is that labor gets simpler when you stop trying to find one perfect cleaner for everything and instead evaluate cleaners by outcomes, fit, and job type. A test clean is a controlled way to answer: does this person do good work, communicate well, take feedback, and meet the final result we promised the client?

With 1099 cleaners, you cannot train them like employees or force the exact tools, products, schedule, or method. What you can do is define the outcome: the bathroom needs to be clean, the floors need to be mopped, the corners need to be hit, and the final result needs to match the client’s expectations. You can strongly recommend the tools and methods you have found work best, but the cleaner is responsible for how they get to the final result.

Pay is also flexible, but the lesson’s general approach is that the business keeps about half and cleaners receive about half, with adjustments based on drive time, job difficulty, lead cleaner versus support cleaner, and whether the job still makes sense for the cleaner. The business money is the owner’s money, but the split needs to make sense for both the business and the cleaner.

When a cleaner misses items, the important question is whether the promised outcome was completed. If the client needs a reclean, the cleaner who missed the work may need to go back and fix it without extra pay because they were paid to complete the job, not simply show up. If a discount has to be given because the job was not completed, the cleaner’s pay can reflect the completed job amount.

The lesson also normalizes that different cleaners have different strengths. A cleaner can be great at recurring standard cleans and not good at deep cleans or move-outs. That does not mean they are useless; it means you need to schedule them into the right type of job and avoid giving them clients or clean types where they are likely to fail.
## Key Principles
- A test clean is for evaluating fit, not promising a long-term spot.
- Judge cleaners by the final outcome they produce.
- With 1099 cleaners, set outcomes and recommendations, not mandatory step-by-step training.
- Not every cleaner should be assigned every type of clean.
- A cleaner who misses the promised outcome should be responsible for fixing it.
- Cleaner pay needs to make sense for both the business and the cleaner.
- A-level clients should get A-level cleaners; easier clients can be good fits for developing cleaners.
- Fear of lower quality should push you to test better, not stay stuck cleaning everything yourself.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Interview the cleaner and verify they seem like a possible fit before giving them work.
2. Run a test clean, ideally with the owner or an established cleaner involved.
3. Evaluate the cleaner by job quality, speed, communication, client interaction, and whether they meet the checklist outcomes.
4. Give feedback as outcome-based notes, not employee-style training or mandatory techniques.
5. Place the cleaner into the clean types they are strongest at, such as recurring standards, deep cleans, or support work.
6. Use a three-strike style internal system for recurring quality or reliability issues.
7. If a clean is missed or incomplete, talk to the client, offer to fix it, and send the responsible cleaner back when appropriate.
8. Adjust cleaner pay based on the completed job, the role they played, drive time, and whether the economics make sense.

- “The end result needs to be that the floor is mopped and there’s nothing on the floor.”
- “We have a satisfaction guarantee, so we’re going to go back out there and fix that thing that we missed.”
- “What can I do? Because I want to make this right for you.”

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Paying a cleaner before confirming the job was completed correctly.
- Trying to force a 1099 cleaner to use an exact product, tool, or method.
- Giving a cleaner a deep clean or move-out clean before proving they can handle it.
- Assuming one missed item means someone cannot be useful anywhere in the business.
- Keeping a bad cleaner because of emotion instead of business logic.
- Failing to do a walkthrough and checklist review before leaving the home.
