# When Should I Get a CRM (how to decide if a CRM is worth it based on leads, follow-up, chaos, and return on cost)

## Best For
Cleaning business owners deciding whether they need a CRM now or can wait until more leads are coming in.
## Problems This Solves
- Texts and leads scattered across Google Voice or personal messages
- Not knowing who has been quoted or needs follow-up
- Having old leads that were never nurtured
- Paying for software before there is enough lead flow
- Not preparing for marketing and scale
## Quick Summary
This lesson frames the CRM decision as a cost-benefit analysis. If spending around $200 helps the business make more than $200, frees up time, and creates follow-up structure, then it is worth it. The speaker says a CRM may not be necessary yet for a solo cleaner with stable recurring clients, no marketing, and few new leads. But if the owner is preparing to grow, investing in marketing, already has leads coming in, has old contacts that said no, or feels like texts and stages are chaotic, a CRM makes sense. A CRM gives stages for where each lead is in the process and helps the team know whether someone was quoted, needs a text, or needs a follow-up. The lesson specifically recommends Harvest CRM and points students to deeper systems training for what to do inside it.
## Core Teaching
The core teaching is that a CRM should be judged by whether it makes the business more money, saves time, and reduces chaos. If spending money on the CRM creates more bookings, captures old leads, and prevents missed follow-up, then it is an investment rather than just a cost.

The lesson gives one clear situation where a CRM may not be needed yet: a solo cleaner with the same recurring clients, no marketing, no new leads, and little pipeline activity. In that case, there may not be enough going on to justify it.

But if the business is preparing to run marketing, grow from the jump, handle more incoming leads, upload old leads, or organize people who previously said no, a CRM makes sense. Even one or two booked cleans from follow-up can justify the tool.

The CRM is also a solution when the business feels chaotic: Google Voice texts everywhere, unclear lead stages, not knowing who was quoted, who needs a text, and who needs follow-up. The CRM gives stages and follow-up systems so the owner knows where everyone is in the process.
## Key Principles
- A CRM should be evaluated by cost versus return.
- If it makes more than it costs and frees time, it is worth considering.
- A solo cleaner with no new leads may not need one yet.
- Marketing and scale require a place to manage leads.
- Old leads can become revenue through CRM follow-up.
- A CRM should show the stage of every lead.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Look at how many new leads are coming in.
2. Look at how many old contacts, no-shows, quotes, and past noes are sitting unused.
3. Ask whether a CRM would likely create more revenue than it costs.
4. Ask whether it would free up meaningful time.
5. If you are running marketing or preparing to scale, set it up earlier.
6. If things feel chaotic across texts and stages, move into a CRM.
7. Use stages to track where every lead is in the process.

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Buying a CRM too early when there are no leads and no follow-up need.
- Waiting too long once marketing and lead flow begin.
- Letting old noes sit without nurture.
- Tracking leads in scattered text threads.
- Not using pipeline stages to decide the next action.
