# When Should I Fire a VA (using KPIs, gut checks, and business logic to know when a VA is not the right fit)

## Problems This Solves
- Getting emotionally attached to a VA who is not performing
- Not knowing what performance metrics to use
- Close rate dropping after handing sales to a VA
- Leads falling through the cracks
- Feeling like you cannot fully trust the VA to run the business
## Quick Summary
Firing a VA is hard because it is easy to build an emotional connection, especially in a small business. The lesson says to use KPIs and business logic instead of only emotion. The speaker gives close rate as a major example: if the owners close around 60% and the VA closes around 20%, that is a serious problem. Other indicators include speed to lead, follow-up discipline, pipeline management, tracking accuracy, customer service quality, and how often the owner has to double-check the VA. The speaker also talks about the gut feeling of not being stoked, feeling like things are slipping, or feeling like the business would fall apart if the owner left for a week. A VA does not have to be better than the owner, but they need to be good enough that the owner trusts them and the numbers support keeping them.
## Core Teaching
The core teaching is that firing a VA is not only an emotional decision. It is a business decision measured by whether the VA is producing the results the role requires.

The lesson uses close rate as a clear KPI. When the owners were closing around 60% and the first VA was closing around 20%, the business was losing money. A VA who takes calls but cannot close, follow up, or manage the pipeline can quietly cap revenue.

Other performance signals include speed to lead, how quickly they call people back, whether they follow up with everyone they should, whether the pipeline stays updated, whether tracking sheets are accurate, and whether upset customers are handled well.

The speaker also acknowledges the gut check. If the owner constantly feels like things are falling through the cracks, has to double-check everything, or cannot leave for a week without expecting pieces to break, the VA may not be the right fit. A VA does not need to be perfect or better than the owner, but the owner should feel confident and genuinely happy with their performance.
## Key Principles
- A VA decision should be based on KPIs, not just emotion.
- Close rate matters when the VA handles sales.
- Speed to lead and follow-up discipline are critical.
- Pipeline accuracy is part of the VA's performance.
- Customer service quality affects reviews and retention.
- A good VA should make the owner feel confident, not nervous.
- Being kind to people does not mean avoiding necessary business decisions.
## Step-by-Step Process
1. Identify the VA's main responsibilities.
2. Measure the KPIs connected to those responsibilities, especially close rate, speed to lead, follow-up, pipeline management, tracking, and customer service.
3. Compare the VA's performance to the owner's baseline or the business target.
4. Look for repeated dropped balls, missed responses, and tracking errors.
5. Notice whether you feel confident letting the VA run things without constant checking.
6. If training has not fixed the core performance issues, make the business decision to let them go.

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Keeping a VA because you like them personally.
- Not tracking close rate or speed to lead.
- Explaining away repeated mistakes instead of looking at the pattern.
- Hiring a VA and then refusing to check performance.
- Waiting until the business has already suffered before making a decision.
