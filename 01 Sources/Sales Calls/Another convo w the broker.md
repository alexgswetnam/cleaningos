# Due Diligence on a Cleaning Biz: Spotting Owner-Dependent Revenue and Fudged Books

## Module
Another convo w the broker

## Course

## Best For

## Problems This Solves
- Not knowing what questions to rapid-fire at a broker on an evaluation call.
- Taking SDE/financials at face value when the owner isn't paying himself for labor he performs.
- Buying a business that secretly depends on the owner's hands-on work and relationships.
- Not understanding client concentration risk (a few clients making up most of the revenue).
- Not knowing how to protect yourself with a non-compete and a real transition period.
- Not understanding seller financing, equity retention, and deal structuring options.
- Confusing deal terms like "balloon" payments.
- Not knowing how to use the due diligence period / LOI to verify claims before committing.

## Quick Summary
This is a real evaluation call between two young buyers and a broker (Dylan) about a 25-year-old commercial cleaning business, followed by the buyers' private debrief. The buyers rapid-fire questions about add-backs, asset condition, client concentration, contracted vs. recurring revenue, and how the owner pays himself. The big red flag that surfaces is that the owner still personally does 15–20 hours a week of one-time cleanup labor — roughly 25% of revenue — but never paid himself for it, which inflates the reported profit. The buyers conclude the books aren't a clean source of truth and that the stated SDE overstates real earnings because someone would have to be hired to replace that labor. They also explore deal structures: seller financing, an SBA loan with a delayed payment window, and letting the seller retain 5–15% equity to lower the purchase price. Throughout, they push for non-competes, a transition period, and a due diligence window where they can verify everything before backing out. The takeaway is to dig past the headline numbers and pressure-test how much of the business actually depends on the owner.

## Core Teaching
When you evaluate a cleaning business to buy, your job is to find out how much of the business is really the owner — his labor, his Rolodex, his relationships — versus a system that runs without him. The headline numbers (SDE, profit) only matter if they reflect reality.

On this call, the buyers go in with an NDA signed and a slideshow/CIM in hand, then rapid-fire questions. They probe the add-backs first, because the add-back amount roughly doubled between 2023 and 2024, and they want to see actual transaction-level detail, not just line summaries. The broker explains much of the discrepancy is a depreciation line, plus the seller ran personal expenses (self-insurance, life insurance, a personal "mountain house" vacation home) through the business.

They check asset condition next — service records on vehicles and equipment, and confirmation that nothing is sitting around broken and unused. The seller is "pen and paper," uses no software, so records are thin and have to be pulled together by hand.

Then comes the most important thread. The business is heavily owner-dependent in a way that wasn't obvious: the owner still personally performs or actively manages the one-time cleanups, which is about 25% of revenue and 15–20 hours of labor a week. Critically, he never paid himself for that labor. That means the reported profit/SDE is overstated, because a new owner who isn't going to do the work would have to hire someone to replace it. The buyers' core question becomes: how much of this revenue and these financials only exist because the owner is working for free?

They also dig into client concentration (do a handful of clients make up most of the revenue?) and contracted vs. recurring work. Most of the recurring work — about 75% of it — has no written contract; it's just longstanding relationships. That's a continuation-of-service risk if the owner leaves and takes the relationships with him. The buyers reference a real cautionary story: Alex's parents bought a business, the seller went and started a competing business, took the employees and clients, and the buyers lost in arbitration and paid the fees. That's why they insist on a non-compete (e.g., a radius and time window) and a clean transition period so the revenue actually transfers.

Finally they explore deal structure. Options on the table: seller financing, an SBA loan with money up front to the seller and a delayed payment window for the buyers, and having the seller retain a small slice of equity (5–15%) in exchange for a lower purchase price. The equity-retention idea is framed as a win-win: the seller gets "mailbox money" and a K-1 that grows as the buyers grow the business, plus upside on a future exit, while the buyers lower their cash outlay and keep the seller lightly aligned as an advisor. They clarify a "balloon" is when you make payments and then pay everything off at the end of the term — not a delayed-start payment, which is what one of them was actually describing.

The debrief lands on the real conclusion: the price made sense on the surface and is "a fair deal," but the books need cleaning before they'll trust them. They want 2025 numbers (not just 2023–2024), clear add-backs, and a real accounting of the owner's unpaid labor. One of them notes a book they're reading lists messy bookkeeping as a *positive* on a "good deal" checklist (because you can come in and clean it up), but they're skeptical and still want a source of truth — likely the tax filings.

## Key Principles
- The headline financials only matter if they reflect reality — verify, don't assume.
- Find out how much of the revenue depends on the owner personally working, not just managing.
- Unpaid owner labor inflates profit/SDE; back out the cost of replacing it.
- Client concentration is a risk: ask whether a few clients make up most of the revenue.
- Recurring revenue without written contracts is relationship-dependent and walks out the door with the owner.
- Always get a non-compete (radius + time) and a real transition period to protect transferred revenue.
- Use the due diligence period after an LOI to actually verify everything before you're locked in.
- Deal structure is flexible: seller financing, SBA, and seller equity retention can lower price and share risk.
- Messy/pen-and-paper bookkeeping can be an opportunity, but you still need a verifiable source of truth.

## Step-by-Step Process
This call models an informal evaluation flow rather than a named framework, but the buyers' approach can be read as steps:
1. Sign the NDA, then request the deal materials (slideshow / CIM) from the broker.
2. Rapid-fire questions to the broker, starting with the financials and add-backs — and ask whether you'll see every transaction or just line summaries.
3. Probe the add-back discrepancy (here, depreciation plus personal expenses run through the business: self-insurance, life insurance, a personal vacation home).
4. Check asset health: service/maintenance records and confirmation equipment is in working order and actively used, not broken and idle.
5. Determine how owner-dependent the revenue is — specifically whether the owner still performs labor and whether he pays himself for it.
6. Back out the cost of replacing any unpaid owner labor to get a true picture of profit.
7. Assess client concentration and the split between contracted and uncontracted recurring revenue.
8. Lock in protections: non-compete (radius + time) and a transition period for clean handoff.
9. Explore deal structures (seller financing, SBA loan with delayed payments, seller equity retention).
10. Sign an LOI and use the due diligence period to verify everything (CPA review, observe day-to-day operations) with the ability to back out.

## Scripts, Examples, or Phrases to Keep
- Opening the call after the NDA: "Would you be able to email that slideshow to us, since we got the NDA signed?"
- Setting the tone for questions: "I'm just going to rapid fire if it's cool with everyone."
- On transaction detail: "Are we going to be able to see basically every single transaction in the business, or are we just going to see line summaries?"
- On asset condition: confirming nothing is "sitting around, and the reason it's not in use is because it doesn't work."
- The non-compete cautionary tale: Alex's parents bought a business, the seller "went off and started another business, took the employees, took the clients," and they lost in arbitration and paid the lawyer fees.
- Framing the non-compete: a radius (e.g., "100, 150 mile radius") so the seller doesn't "go rip the shirt off that they just sold."
- The client concentration question: "Do we have 50 clients, but four of them make up 40% of the revenue?"
- The unpaid-labor insight: he's doing "15 to 20 hours a week of labor" on one-time cleanups, but "he wasn't paying himself for the work that he was doing," so the books are fudged.
- "You can't say 128 SDE or whatever when you didn't pay for the labor of 25% of that."
- The equity-retention pitch: a small percentage (5–15%) where "the purchase price gets wiggly and he gets a K-1 that grows every year because we're growing the business," plus upside on a future exit — "mailbox money."
- The buyers' self-positioning: "We excel in growth. We excel in sales and marketing." Buying a business is like buying "a house that looks like it could use a pretty good facelift."
- Definition correction: a balloon is "where you pay everything at that time" / make payments then pay it all off at the end — not a delayed-start payment.

## Common Mistakes / Warnings
- Taking SDE or profit at face value when the owner performs unpaid labor — it overstates earnings.
- Assuming "the owner just does admin/management" when he's actually doing 15–20 hours of hands-on labor a week.
- Ignoring that one person being the only one trained/trusted to do a high-margin task (the one-time cleanups, ~25% of revenue) is a concentration risk.
- Buying recurring revenue that has no written contracts and lives entirely in the owner's relationships.
- Skipping a non-compete — the seller can take employees and clients and start over (the arbitration cautionary tale).
- Trusting incomplete records; the seller is pen-and-paper with no software, so numbers must be pulled together by hand and verified.
- Only looking at 2023–2024 — they want 2025 too, plus clear add-backs.
- The broker not proactively disclosing the owner's labor hours until pressed.
- Misusing deal terminology (confusing a "balloon" with a delayed-payment structure).

## Cleaned Lesson Notes

**Setup.** Buyers (Alex, Elijah, with Courtney and Oliver) get on a call with broker Dylan about a 25-year-old commercial cleaning business. NDA is signed; they request the slideshow/CIM. Alex runs rapid-fire questions.

**Add-backs.** Add-backs roughly doubled from 2023 to 2024. Buyers want to know if they can see every transaction or just line summaries. Dylan says the big discrepancy is mostly a depreciation line, plus the owner ran personal expenses through the business — self-insurance, life insurance, and a personal "mountain house" vacation home (no business use, no supplies/equipment stored there).

**Asset health.** Buyers ask for service/maintenance records on vehicles and equipment and confirmation everything is in working order and actively used. The owner is decent about maintenance but pen-and-paper, so full service dates aren't all recorded. One spare truck is on standby; burners, cleaners, and hoses are reportedly in active use.

**Owner dependency (the key issue).** The business has ~8 long-tenured employees, but the owner still personally does or actively manages the one-time cleanups — about 25% of revenue and 15–20 hours of labor per week (sometimes more). He's the only one he trusts to do them ("no one cleans better than me"). Crucially, he did NOT pay himself for that labor. So a new owner who won't do the work has to hire someone to replace it, which lowers the true profit. The reported SDE overstates real earnings. Buyers want clarity on his salary, how he draws money (believed S-corp), and how much of the payments came from him working the jobs.

**Client concentration & contracts.** Buyers ask how many clients there are and whether a few make up most of the revenue. Dylan doesn't have exact figures — the owner's pen-and-paper, so it has to be assembled. About 75% of revenue is recurring (weekly/bi-weekly), but most of it has no written contract; only a few buildings (e.g., the New Brunswick office) are contracted. The rest is longstanding relationship-based recurring work — a continuation risk if the owner leaves.

**Protections.** Buyers want a non-compete (radius + time, e.g., 100–150 miles) and a real transition period so revenue transfers cleanly. Motivation: Alex's parents bought a business where the seller started a competitor, took the clients and employees, and the parents lost in arbitration and paid the fees. Dylan says the seller is open to a non-compete and transition and prefers to walk away cleanly.

**Deal structure.** Options discussed: seller financing (seller wants to move in ~6–12 months), an SBA loan giving the seller money up front with a delayed payment window for the buyers, and the seller retaining a small equity stake (5–15%) in exchange for a lower purchase price. The equity-retention idea is pitched as win-win: seller gets passive income, a growing K-1, and upside on a future exit; buyers cut the cash price (potentially $50–100K+ off). They clarify a "balloon" means making payments then paying everything off at the end of the term — not a delayed-start payment.

**Next steps / due diligence.** Buyers want an LOI followed by a due diligence period where they can verify everything (CPA review, observing the owner run a job site day-to-day) and still back out. They position themselves as growth/sales/marketing operators (they grew a house cleaning business from zero to $27,500/month in about a year using VAs, CRMs, AI, and automations) who want to give this business a "facelift."

**Debrief conclusion.** On the surface it's a fair deal — and now the price makes sense — but the books aren't a clean source of truth. The owner is "missing some pretty big stuff." They need 2025 numbers (not just 2023–2024), clearly stated add-backs, and an honest accounting of the unpaid owner labor that fudges the SDE. One buyer notes a book they're reading lists bad bookkeeping as a positive on a "good deal" checklist (you can come in and clean it up), but they're skeptical and still want to find the real source of truth — possibly the tax filings.
