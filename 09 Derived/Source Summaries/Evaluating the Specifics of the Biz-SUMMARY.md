## Core Teaching
This lesson is about reading the deal's specifics critically rather than taking the marketing materials at face value. The buyers walk the CIM/PowerPoint slide by slide and pressure-test each claim.

**Systems the business lacks (and the upside).** The seller has no booking system and creates/sends invoices manually. The buyers see this as opportunity: put all the W2 employees into a booking system (BookingKoala) so they get steady, predictable schedules — important because it's commercial work. The one gap is that BookingKoala may not handle quarterly/biannual/annual frequencies or comprehensive invoicing out of the box; one buyer has previously figured out how to build tiers in BookingKoala by experimenting, but Jobber may be the better fit for more comprehensive scheduling and invoicing. For the owner's "fill-in when someone calls out" role, the buyer would set up an on-call backup system (like a waiter asking someone to cover a shift) rather than personally cleaning — though he could do it himself if needed.

**Add-backs.** An add-back is an expense the seller removes from the financials to show higher profit, on the logic that the expense won't exist for the buyer. Example: if paper profit is $60K but the owner ran $20K/year of mountain-house expense through the business, they add it back to show ~$80K SDE. The danger is that add-backs can be manipulated to inflate SDE and therefore the asking price. Here the add-backs roughly doubled to about $63K in a single year (partly more depreciation on mixed-use assets, plus health insurance and personal lifestyle expenses run through the business — common in small service businesses). Because the jump is large for a 25-year-old business, the buyers want an actual line-item breakdown of the add-backs to confirm nothing is inflated. They also note this is a no-debt sale (the owner retains any debt), which is good, and that a tax clearance / certificate of no tax due would protect the buyer from inheriting the seller's tax liabilities.

**Financial anomalies.** 2023 and 2024 revenue are within a few hundred dollars of each other, yet SDE jumps ~$15K. That low variance with a profit jump is unusual and worth a real revenue breakdown. 2025 isn't finished yet, so they want those numbers too.

**Owner hours.** The CIM says the owner works 25–40 hours/week, but on the call the broker/owner indicated it's about 20 hours, roughly half admin and half filling in when guys call out. The buyers debate the discrepancy: one argues padding the number high is smart ("under-promise, over-deliver") so a new owner isn't blindsided; the other would rather state the honest range (e.g., 20–40 with context). Either way, the owner personally performs and manages a meaningful chunk of variable one-time revenue.

**The contract risk (the central issue).** The business has no written contracts — revenue is recurring out of habit and relationships, and the commercial clients are effectively month-to-month. This matters enormously: a contracted $300K business gives a buyer near-certainty of next year's revenue, while a relationship-only business offers no guarantee — "anything can happen." It drastically changes the value. The buyers reference the owner having built "a book of relationships," and note that since contracts are standard in commercial cleaning, the absence of them is leverage to push back on price (though the seller may claim it's already priced in).

**The cautionary tale.** One buyer recounts his parents buying a close friend's jiu-jitsu gym. They took it from 30% to 50% to 100% ownership, grew it, and started succeeding where the original owner couldn't. The former owner got resentful, opened the same business 10 miles away despite a non-compete, and won in arbitration (an arbitrator, essentially a retired judge; her lawyer worked on contingency). The parents spent about $300K, had wages garnished, paid her money and both sides' legal fees, lost students and instructors, and after nine years netted about $20K. The lesson: in a relationship-heavy, no-contract business, your relationship with the seller during the transition is paramount, and contracts are extremely important — people will undermine the very business they sold you.

**The fix and the structure.** Since the seller has had these relationships for years, the buyers want to ask him to get contracts in place before/at the sale ("you're already doing it now"). On financing, one buyer would do seller financing rather than an SBA loan — he isn't dependent on the income (his own company covers his living), so this would be "extra," and he wouldn't do the deal at all if seller financing weren't available. They'd also want to know client concentration (e.g., if two accounts make up 50% of revenue, that's a big deal) before going further.

## Key Principles
- Read the CIM slide by slide and verify each claim — don't trust the marketing.
- Add-backs can be legitimate, but they can also inflate SDE; always get a line-item breakdown.
- A large, sudden jump in add-backs on an old business is a flag worth investigating.
- Revenue that's nearly flat year over year while SDE jumps is an anomaly — get the real breakdown.
- No written contracts = relationship-only revenue = drastically lower certainty and value.
- In commercial cleaning, contracts are standard; their absence is leverage to push back on price.
- Your relationship with the seller during the transition is paramount in a relationship-heavy business.
- A non-compete on paper isn't bulletproof — sellers can still compete and even win in arbitration.
- You don't need to personally know how to do every service line; you can have employees handle it.
- Protect against inherited liabilities with a no-debt sale and a tax clearance certificate.
- Prefer a financing structure you can walk away from; don't do the deal if the terms don't work.

## Step-by-Step Process
This is a deal-review session rather than a named framework, but the buyers' approach maps to:
1. Get the CIM/PowerPoint and walk through it slide by slide.
2. Identify missing systems (booking, invoicing, frequency tiers) and plan what you'd implement (BookingKoala / possibly Jobber) and how you'd cover the owner's fill-in role (on-call backups).
3. Decode the add-backs: confirm what each one is and whether it legitimately won't exist for you.
4. Demand a line-item add-back breakdown, especially where add-backs spiked year over year.
5. Check for financial anomalies (flat revenue with jumping SDE) and request a real revenue breakdown plus 2025 numbers.
6. Scrutinize stated owner hours against what was said on the call.
7. Assess contract risk — written contracts vs. relationship/habit-based recurring revenue.
8. Plan to get contracts put in place and to prioritize the relationship with the seller through transition.
9. Ask for client concentration data (how many accounts, what share of revenue each).
10. Choose a deal structure you're comfortable with (seller financing preferred over SBA here) and be willing to walk if it doesn't work.
11. Schedule the next meeting and, eventually, a call directly with the owner.

- Cutting a service line (e.g., construction cleanup ≈ 25% of revenue, ~$70K) just because you personally can't do it — you can have employees handle it.
