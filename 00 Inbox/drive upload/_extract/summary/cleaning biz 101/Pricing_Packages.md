## Core Teaching
Most scheduling chaos starts with pricing chaos. Your team should not be guessing with prices — creating a quote should be systemized. And the goal with pricing isn't perfection; the goal is consistency.

At a high level, a pricing system should include a base price model, add-ons, a recurring discount structure (if you choose to offer one), and minimums so small jobs don't drain you. Pricing systems protect both your profit and your sanity.

It's impossible to tell you what your specific pricing should be, but here's the method used in this business:

**Flat rate reverse-engineered from an hourly target.** They charge a flat rate, and to get it they reverse-engineer their target hourly rate — they want to charge $60/hr per cleaner. So if a 3/2, 1800 sqft monthly clean for a couple with one kid takes 3 hours on average, they charge $179 + tax. Another example: a 3000 sqft deep clean that averages 10 hours, at a target of $55/hr, would be a $550 clean. Some jobs take longer and some shorter, but the result for the client is the same — a satisfied, clean home.

**Find your cleaners' pace.** Every cleaner works at a different speed. To determine how long each cleaner takes, they keep a spreadsheet and figure out each one's sqft/hr. Do this for a month and you'll find your average.

**Add a condition parameter.** Eventually they added a "condition of the home" pricing parameter. A home at dirtiness level 2 takes far less time than one at level 7, so condition adjusts the price.

Everyone prices differently. A Pricing 101 course module is coming soon.

## Step-by-Step Process
The lesson shares a repeatable pricing method:
1. Set a target hourly rate per cleaner (the example uses $60/hr).
2. Estimate how long a given job takes on average (based on home size, type of clean, occupants, etc.).
3. Multiply the average hours by your target hourly rate to get the flat rate, then add tax (e.g., a 3-hour clean → $179 + tax; a 10-hour deep clean at $55/hr → $550).
4. Track each cleaner's sqft/hr in a spreadsheet for about a month to determine their average pace.
5. Use those averages to estimate job times more accurately going forward.
6. Add a "condition of the home" parameter (e.g., a dirtiness scale) so dirtier homes are priced for the extra time.
