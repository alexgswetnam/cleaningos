# Pricing Methodology

> [!abstract] In one line
> Reverse-engineer a flat rate from a target hourly rate and a measured time-per-job — consistency, not perfection, is the goal.

## Definition

A systematic way to set flat-rate prices: start from a target hourly rate per cleaner,
measure how long a job of a given size and condition actually takes, and multiply.
Distinct from [[Price Delivery]] (how to *say* the price on a call) and [[Price
Objection]] (how to defend it) — this is how the number gets built in the first place.

## Why It Matters

Most scheduling chaos starts with pricing chaos. If a team is guessing at prices on every
call, quoting becomes inconsistent, jobs get mis-scheduled for the time they actually
take, and margins erode invisibly. A quoting system protects both profit and sanity —
and it's what makes [[Sales Pipeline Stages]]'s Quoted stage fast and confident rather
than improvised.

## Symptoms

- Different quotes for similar jobs depending on who's on the phone
- Jobs consistently running over or under their scheduled time
- Not knowing actual profit per clean because the price wasn't built from a real cost basis

## Common Mistakes

- Treating pricing as a one-time guess instead of a system with inputs (base price,
  add-ons, recurring discount structure, minimums)
- Not setting a minimum, letting small jobs drain time relative to what they pay
- Assuming a single sqft/hour rate applies to every cleaner instead of measuring each one
- Ignoring home condition as a real driver of time, and therefore price

## Models

**The core method: reverse-engineer the hourly rate.** Decide a target hourly rate per
cleaner (the source's own target: $60/hour). Measure how long a representative job
actually takes (e.g. a 3-bed/2-bath, 1,800 sqft monthly clean for a couple with one kid
averages 3 hours). Multiply: $60 × 3 = $179 + tax as the flat rate for that job profile.
A bigger, less frequent job scales the same way — a 3,000 sqft deep clean averaging 10
hours at a $55/hour target becomes a $550 clean. The client experiences a flat number
either way; the business is pricing off real time, not a guess.

**Measure actual pace per cleaner, not an assumed average.** Every cleaner works at a
different pace. Track sqft/hour per cleaner in a spreadsheet for about a month to find
each one's real average, rather than pricing the whole team off one assumed rate.

**Home condition is a real pricing input, not an edge case.** A "condition of the home"
parameter (e.g. a 1–10 dirtiness scale) materially changes time required — a level-2 home
takes far less time than a level-7 home of the same size. Building this into the pricing
system avoids either underpricing dirty homes or overpricing clean ones.

**The four building blocks of a pricing system:** a base price model, add-ons, a
recurring-discount structure (if used), and minimums so small jobs don't drain the
business. The goal across all four is consistency, not a theoretically perfect number —
there's no universal answer for what a given business *should* charge.

## Checklist

- [ ] Set a target hourly rate per cleaner
- [ ] Measure actual time for representative job profiles (size, bed/bath, frequency)
- [ ] Track sqft/hour per individual cleaner, not a team-wide assumption
- [ ] Add a home-condition parameter to adjust for dirtiness level
- [ ] Define a base price, add-ons, a recurring discount (if any), and a minimum
- [ ] Re-measure periodically rather than treating the first pricing pass as permanent

## FAQ

**Q:** What should I actually charge?
**A:** Not answerable in general — explicitly stated as impossible to prescribe. The
method (target hourly rate × measured time) is transferable; the resulting number is
specific to each business's market and cost structure.
