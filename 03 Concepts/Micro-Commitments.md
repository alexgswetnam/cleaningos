# Micro-Commitments

> [!abstract] In one line
> Get ten small yeses before the big one, so the price is a confirmation instead of the start of a negotiation.

## Definition

A chain of small agreements — the day, the time, the gate code — collected *before* you say
the number. By the time the price arrives, the prospect has mentally booked.

## Why It Matters

Where the price sits in the call determines what the price *is*.

Go straight from "what's your square footage" to "here's the price" and the number is the
first real decision they've made. It's the opening bid in a negotiation, and everything
after it is defence.

Collect the day, the time, and the logistics first, and the number is the last small step in
something already underway. Nothing about the price changed. What changed is that they've
already pictured Thursday at 2 PM.

This is [[Five Laws|Build In Order]] inside a single phone call.

## Symptoms

- The price is the first thing the prospect has to agree to
- "Let me think about it" arrives immediately after the number — see [[Handling Let Me Think About It]]
- You quote, then start negotiating, every single time
- You know the square footage but not what day they want

## Common Mistakes

- Quoting as soon as you have enough information to quote
- Asking for the specs and the price in the same breath
- Skipping the availability check — it's what makes the slot feel scarce
- Collecting commitments and then *still* delivering the price as a question. See
  [[Price Delivery]]; the two failures compound.
- Running the chain mechanically on someone who's already ready to book

## Models

**The chain.** Each link is a yes, and each one is easy:

```
"What day would work best for you?"          → "Thursday or Friday."
"Let me check if we even have availability
 — we're pretty packed."                     → (scarcity, and a pause)
"I've got this Thursday at 2 PM — that work?" → "Yes."
"Morning or afternoon?"                       → "Afternoon."
"Gated community, or do we need a code?"      → "It's gated, here's the code."
        ↓
Recap everything back
        ↓
"It's just going to be $180 plus tax."
```

**The availability check does two jobs.** It creates scarcity — *we're pretty packed* — and
it converts the next question from "would you like to book?" into "can you make this slot?"

**The recap.** *"So we've got 3 bed, 2 bath, 2,000 square feet, Thursday at 2 PM."* Saying
it back makes the booking feel like it already exists, and it catches errors before they
become disputes.

**Then the assumed close**, immediately, with no pause for permission:

> "It's going to be $180 plus tax. We don't charge until after the clean because we want to
> make sure you're satisfied. Whenever you're ready, I can grab your card."

The payment-terms sentence is doing quiet work — it removes the last risk in the same breath
as the number.

## Checklist

- [ ] Finish diagnosing before starting the chain — see [[Diagnose Don't Take Orders]]
- [ ] Day
- [ ] Availability check out loud
- [ ] Specific slot, offered
- [ ] Morning or afternoon
- [ ] Access logistics — gate, key, code
- [ ] Recap all of it back to them
- [ ] Price, with downward inflection
- [ ] Assumed close, no pause

## FAQ

**Q:** Isn't it dishonest to say I'm packed if I'm not?
**A:** Then don't say it. The availability *check* still works — pausing to look is real, and
it reframes the slot as something you're offering rather than something they're requesting.

**Q:** What if they ask the price up front?
**A:** Give a range, then keep going: *"Usually somewhere around $170 to $200 for a place
that size — let me get a couple of details and I'll tell you exactly. What day works best
for you?"*
