# Managing A VA

> [!abstract] In one line
> Measure availability, not activity — and never invent busy work to fill their hours.

## Definition

How to onboard and manage a virtual assistant without surveillance theatre.

## Why It Matters

The instinct is to monitor. Screen sharing, activity trackers, filling their day. It
damages trust and measures the wrong thing.

> "I really don't mind if Elle is working 30 minutes a day or if she's working 12 hours a
> day. As long as she's doing the job and she's doing it well."

## Symptoms

- Your VA pushes back on screen sharing
- You're checking whether they're "working" rather than whether work is done
- You're inventing tasks to justify their hours
- Response times are slipping and you don't have a standard

## Common Mistakes

- **Screen recording as a permanent practice.** Reasonable for the first week or two of
  onboarding. Not forever. It also genuinely slows their machine — *"you got to think they
  don't have a super nice computer."* Both Elle and a previous VA found it frustrating.
- **Busy work.** Explicitly rejected:
  > "I'm not going to give you extra tasks just to be busy. I'm not going to give you busy
  > work. Unless there's something that is going to drive results, drive revenue, I'm not
  > going to throw it on your plate. I'm not going to say, actually, I kind of want the
  > colors on the website to look different. It's just stupid."
- **Monitoring without saying why.** If the real goal is availability, say so — and then
  solve for availability instead.
- **Under-scoping the role.** The opposite failure to busy work, and easier to miss because
  it looks like restraint. The VA is handed one narrow task in one area when they could
  carry a function. Nothing looks wrong — the work gets done — and the capacity you paid
  for never arrives. Ask what they could own, not what you can safely hand over.

## Models

**Treat the lead VA like an equity partner, not a task robot.** Give them buy-in to
decisions. Ask about their goals, life, situation — get to know them as a person, not just
a function.

> "I almost treat them and respect them in a way that's as if they're an equity partner.
> When someone has input into a decision, they own that decision."

Project a future with them: *"We want to grow. You're part of that. One day you could have
a team of VAs under you."* Alex's own VA loves playing Roblox on weekends, is always by
her computer, and wants to earn more for her family — knowing that shapes how he manages
her.

**Pay structure: start low, bump often, surprise-bonus the big days.** Start at $6/hour.
Do small, frequent bumps rather than one big raise later — a month in, bump to $6.50, then
$7, then $7.50 over time:

> "Start six. A month in, bump to six fifty. They're getting that dopamine hit. They're
> working towards that next bump."

Give surprise bonuses for standout performance, with no precedent set: a VA closed five or
six people in one day, Alex sent $80 unprompted — *"You killed it. I want you to keep
killing it."* An optional incentive structure layers a base rate (e.g. $200/week) plus a
per-booking and per-review bonus (e.g. $10 each) — estimate what it looks like on both the
bad and good end before offering it. Not every VA wants variable pay: some genuinely
prefer steady income over an incentive structure, so ask rather than assume.

**The cleaner-to-VA communication transition needs a bridge, not a cutoff.** Cleaners are
used to texting the owner directly. Don't just disappear when a VA takes over
communication — check in with them personally (pull up to a job, grab coffee, call them):
*"How's it going? Is the VA taking care of you? Do you have any issues?"* Make the
message explicit:

> "I'm not handing off the responsibility of making sure you're happy. You can still
> message me. But the VA will handle day-to-day client communications and scheduling."

Over time the VA earns trust and the owner naturally steps back — Alex hasn't personally
talked to a single cleaner in months, except when one asked for career advice. If a
cleaner comments on the VA's accent or asks where they're from, a simple, matter-of-fact
answer is usually enough: *"They're from another country — that explains it."*

**First week: follow, then do, then role-play.** Have the VA follow you through every task
first — calls, bookings, pipeline management. Then have them do it while you watch:
*"Does that make sense? Show me how you'd do this. Let's role play a call."* It's
mentorship, not documentation-first. The VA takes on the owner's approach based on how
they're trained — a customer-centric, curious owner trains a customer-centric, curious VA.
Structure the actual day around the [[SOPs|daily checklist]] so the VA has a concrete
routine from day one, not just shadowing.

**The first VA will feel clunky — you're building the role, not filling a predefined
one.** Spend the first two to three weeks being deliberate about three questions: what
does the VA own each day, what do you keep, and when do they escalate to you. Not having
answers yet is normal; not asking the questions is the mistake.

**The $50 rule for decision escalation.** If a decision costs under $50, the VA makes it
without asking. If it's a policy question that affects the future rather than today, it
goes into the weekly review — not a text that pulls the owner in mid-day:

> "If you need to ask about a $50 decision, don't ask. If it's under $50, you can do
> whatever you want."
>
> "If you feel like you have a question about a policy for the future, that's a
> conversation we have on the weekly review. I don't need a text at 4 PM."

**A standing weekly review call is where policy actually gets decided.** Set a recurring
call (e.g. Friday) where the VA brings what they think needs to change — process
suggestions, policy questions, friction they hit that week. That's the forum for
decisions; real time is for execution.

**A daily 15–30 minute debrief, especially early on.** "How did the day go? What did you
do? What questions do you have?" This is what creates the "rubbing shoulders" effect
that's easy to lose in a fully remote relationship — more contact between the owner and a
new VA accelerates how fast they get competent. Some owners structure their whole day
around this proximity: Alex and a fellow member keep offices next to each other for the
same reason, in person.

**Delegate by asking what the business needs, not what you feel like doing.** The test:
what does the business need? Then, can someone else do it? Tasks an owner emotionally
avoids — updating the lead sheet, rescheduling calls, fixing double bookings — are
frequently exactly what a low-cost VA should own:

> "What does the business need? Put emotions aside. If the business needs it and you hate
> it, pay someone else to do it. Make your VA's life easier too."

A VA at roughly $5/hour can absorb tasks an owner is avoiding purely out of dislike, not
because they require the owner specifically.

**Gender shows up in close rate, for sales-VA roles specifically.** Not a hiring
requirement, but a named, repeated pattern: Alex's main VA (female) closes at 45–55%,
against Kyle (male, 30%). *"Women want to talk to women"* is the stated read, with male
VAs sometimes getting *"you're not cleaning my house, are you?"* pushback on calls. Worth
weighing when staffing a phone-sales role specifically — it doesn't apply to VA roles
without a sales-conversation component.

**The VA placement pipeline, end to end.** Train the VA first → a 30-day trial with the
member, no charge → if it's a good fit, a 12-month commitment through the school, with
SOPs included from day one. A lead VA (Elle, in this source) sources and vets candidates
for other members — including, in one case, firing her own friend for not performing,
named as evidence of how seriously the vetting is taken.

**Discord as the office.** The VA sits in a voice channel during work hours. You drop in
whenever. That's presence without surveillance.

> "Our VAs go into Discord, a group chat, a voice chat — and they're in office. So they're
> in that chat, and I can jump in that chat at any point."

Used for the first two weeks to a month of training. After that it isn't needed.

**The one metric: response time.**

> "I've never had her take more than literally 30 seconds to respond. If your VA is taking
> three to five minutes to respond every single time — I mean, they might be in the
> bathroom, whatever. But if it's every single time, how often are they really checking?"

**The one acceptable exception:** *"I'm on the phone with a client."* Alex: *"that's really
the only excuse for ever not picking up."*

## Checklist

- [ ] Weeks 1–2: screen share or activity tracking, framed as temporary onboarding
- [ ] Say explicitly why, and when it ends
- [ ] Move to Discord voice channel as the "office"
- [ ] Set the standard: respond within 30 seconds during work hours
- [ ] Only exception: on a call with a client
- [ ] Never assign work that doesn't drive results
- [ ] Once trust is established, drop the channel — just call
- [ ] Start pay at $6/hour with small, frequent bumps rather than one big raise
- [ ] Give unprompted surprise bonuses for standout days
- [ ] Ask whether they prefer steady pay or an incentive structure — don't assume
- [ ] Personally check in with cleaners during the communication handoff; don't disappear
- [ ] First week: they follow you, then they do it while you watch, then role-play
- [ ] Anchor the first week to the daily checklist SOP, not just shadowing
- [ ] Spend the first 2–3 weeks deliberately defining what the VA owns vs. what you keep
- [ ] Set the $50 rule: under $50, VA decides; policy questions wait for the weekly review
- [ ] Set a standing weekly review call for policy and process decisions
- [ ] Do a daily 15–30 minute debrief with a new VA, especially in the first weeks

## Templates

> "We're trying to on-ramp you. We want to see what your workflow looks like, and it's only
> temporary — probably a week of this. I know it's intensive."

> "I'm not going to give you extra tasks just to be busy."

> "We want to grow. You're part of that. One day you could have a team of VAs under you."

> "Is the VA taking care of you? You can still message me. But for the most part, they're
> going to be our team lead."

## Student Examples

**Jack** — his VA Eva pushed back: *"I don't like that you're making me share my screen as
I'm working. I feel like it's a little too much."* Jack also felt bad he couldn't give her
full attention (8–9am, then work, then handoff). Alex's read: figure out what the screen
share is actually for. If it's availability, use Discord instead.

**Alex** — used activity tracking for the first week with Elle and a previous VA, Alan.
The difference in output was visible and informative. It was still frustrating for Elle.
He no longer uses it.

**Alex, 2026-04-15** — catches himself under-scoping a VA hired that same day.

> [!quote] Alex, [[2026-04-15 Weekly Coaching Call]]
> "We just hired another virtual assistant today, so it's like, okay, like, that one, I'm
> like, just do this one thing in this area. But I'm like, no, like, he could be, like, an
> assistant. Like, he could do more."

> [!quote] Alex, [[2026-04-15 Weekly Coaching Call]]
> "I'm looking at the thing I wrote down, which is every leader underestimates their team's
> potential. And I'm like, yeah, I think I'm underestimating my team's potential."

He names trust as the constraint rather than the VA's capability — he had avoided handing
over video editing because *"they're going to do it wrong… they're not going to do how I
like it."* The fix he lands on is not a better VA. It's giving the existing one more.

**Alex, 2026-03-18** — described his own VA's pay progression ($6 → $6.50 → $7 → $7.50
over several months) and an unprompted $80 bonus after she closed five or six people in a
single day. Also described the cleaner-transition check-ins he still does personally,
months after handing off day-to-day communication.

**Rick, 2026-04-01** — automated the lead sheet with a Zapier webhook fired only by an
"update contact" tag, cutting monthly zap volume from 1,500 to 200 and reducing cost — a
task Rick had been avoiding manually maintaining. See [[Zapier]].

**Rick and Melissa, 2026-04-08** — onboarding their VA Grace: a two-hour kickoff call to
get her logged into every system, handed the one-page sales SOP, and her first assignment
was calling every recent booking to check how the clean went — a concrete, low-risk first
task rather than open-ended shadowing.
