# Sell The Result Not The Service

> [!abstract] In one line
> Nobody buys "we mop floors and clean baseboards" — they buy relief, pride, and a home they're not embarrassed by.

## Definition

The claim that marketing copy, sales scripts, and follow-up messages should describe the
emotional or practical outcome of a clean, not the checklist of tasks performed. Distinct
from [[Differentiation And Trust Building]], which is about *why choose this company* —
this page is about *why buy this service at all*.

## Why It Matters

A prospect isn't comparing task lists in their head. They're weighing a feeling: relief
from dog hair everywhere, not being embarrassed when in-laws show up, one less thing to
fight about with a spouse, being able to sit in a clean home without stress. Marketing that
lists "we do these 72 things" never touches any of that, and reads like every competitor's
copy because every competitor is listing the same tasks.

## Symptoms

- Ads, website copy, and sales scripts describe cleaning tasks rather than outcomes
- Copy sounds interchangeable with every other cleaning company's copy
- Sales calls explain the process instead of asking about the prospect's actual pain

## Common Mistakes

- Writing "we do these 72 things in your home" as the pitch
- Assuming customers care about technical process details the way the owner does
- Missing obvious emotional pain points: pet hair, embarrassment before guests, marital
  tension over an undone chore, lack of time
- Forgetting that the satisfaction guarantee is part of the *outcome* promise, not a
  separate feature

## Models

**The TurboTax analogy.** TurboTax doesn't advertise its buttons and steps — it sells
"your taxes are done, easily, without stress." The customer buys the outcome, not the
software mechanics.

**The Cancun-flight analogy.** A travel company sells the vacation — the beach, the food,
the escape from work — not the TSA line and boarding process. The process is what the
company handles; the result is what gets sold.

**Apply it to cleaning: sell what the clean removes, not what it does.** Relief from dog
hair, not being embarrassed when friends or in-laws visit, one less source of tension with
a spouse, being able to sit in a clean home without stress. Even semi-playful framing
("this will save your marriage") works because it names a real felt pain rather than a
task.

**The satisfaction guarantee belongs to this framing, not a separate feature list.** It
supports the promise that the customer will be *happy with the result* — reinforcing the
outcome sale rather than sitting as an isolated bullet point.

## Checklist

- [ ] Identify what the prospect is actually stressed about, not just what room needs
      cleaning
- [ ] Rewrite each service-feature bullet as the outcome or feeling it produces
- [ ] Use recognizable pain points: pets, guests, time pressure, spousal tension
- [ ] Reference the satisfaction guarantee as part of the outcome promise, not a bolted-on
      feature
- [ ] Read the copy back and ask: does this describe a task, or a result?

## Templates

- "Let someone else take care of it so you can sit at home happy in a clean place, not
  feeling judged."
- (Semi-playful, use with judgment) "This will save your marriage by having a cleaning."

> When drafting ad copy, website copy, or a follow-up message, check every sentence for

> rewrite it as the feeling or result that task produces before using it.
