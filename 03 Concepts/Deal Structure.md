# Deal Structure

> [!abstract] In one line
> The price is one number and the deal is many. How you pay is as negotiable as what you pay.

> [!warning] This page describes financing arrangements, not advice
> Everything here is drawn from one recorded conversation between buyers and a broker. It is
> a record of options discussed, not a recommendation. Any actual deal needs a lawyer and an
> accountant.

## Definition

How the purchase gets paid for: how much cash up front, who lends, over what period, and
what the seller keeps.

## Why It Matters

Buyers fixate on the price and negotiate it hardest, when structure often moves more. A
lower cash outlay, a delayed first payment, or a seller who stays partly invested can change
the risk of a deal more than a discount would.

Structure is also where alignment gets built. A seller with retained equity has a reason to
want you to succeed after closing — which matters a great deal when the revenue is
relationship-held. See [[Uncontracted Recurring Revenue]].

## Symptoms

- The negotiation has become a single number moving up and down
- You need all the cash on day one and don't have it
- The seller wants out but is worried about the buyer's ability to run it
- You've been told a deal doesn't work when what doesn't work is the structure

## Common Mistakes

- Negotiating price only
- Misusing the vocabulary. A **balloon** is when you make payments and then pay everything
  off at the end of the term — *not* a delayed start to payments. Confusing the two produces
  a negotiation where the parties are describing different deals.
- Taking a delayed payment window without knowing whether the business can carry the payment
  when it starts
- Giving away equity without agreeing what rights come with it

## Models

**Three options discussed in the source:**

| Structure | How it works | Who it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Seller financing** | Seller is paid over time out of the business | Buyer's cash outlay; seller's timeline (here: wanted to move in ~6–12 months) |
| **SBA loan with a delayed payment window** | Seller paid up front by the lender; buyer's payments start later | Seller gets certainty; buyer gets runway before debt service starts |
| **Seller retains equity (5–15%)** | Purchase price drops; seller keeps a minority stake | Both — see below |

**The equity-retention pitch, in the buyers' own framing:**

> The purchase price "gets wiggly" and he gets "a K-1 that grows every year because we're
> growing the business," plus upside on a future exit — "mailbox money."

What each side gets:

| Seller | Buyer |
|---|---|
| Passive income | Lower cash price — potentially $50–100K+ off |
| A K-1 that grows if the business grows | Seller stays lightly aligned as an advisor |
| Upside on a future exit | Seller has a reason to want the transition to work |

The last row is the real one. A seller with 10% left has an interest in you keeping the
clients he introduced you to.

**The vocabulary, corrected:**

> A balloon is "where you pay everything at that time" — you make payments through the term
> and then pay the remaining balance off at the end. A delayed-start payment is a different
> thing entirely.

**A second buyer pair's stated preference: seller financing over SBA, as a hard
condition.** A likely companion session states the preference plainly rather than
weighing trade-offs: prefer seller financing to an SBA loan, specifically because the
buyer isn't dependent on the acquisition's income (his own existing business already
covers his living, so this purchase would be "extra") — and he wouldn't do the deal at
all if seller financing weren't available. This is a concrete instance of the general
principle already on this page: prefer a structure you can walk away from, and know your
own cash position before proposing anything.

## Checklist

- [ ] Establish the seller's actual timeline and motivation before proposing anything
- [ ] Ask what he'd take with seller financing versus all cash
- [ ] Model whether the business services the debt when payments begin
- [ ] If proposing retained equity: agree the percentage, the rights, and the exit terms
- [ ] Use the terminology precisely, and confirm both sides mean the same thing
- [ ] Lawyer and accountant before signing anything

## Templates

> "Would he consider retaining a small percentage — say 5 to 15% — in exchange for a lower
> purchase price? He'd get a K-1 that grows as we grow it, plus upside if we sell later."

## AI Prompts

> Do not recommend a financing structure. Lay out the options, name the trade-offs, and say
> plainly that structuring a purchase requires a lawyer and an accountant. If asked which is
> best, answer that it depends on the seller's timeline and the buyer's cash position, and
> ask about both.

## FAQ

**Q:** Which structure is best?
**A:** Depends on the seller's timeline and your cash position. The source discusses options
and reaches no conclusion — no deal had closed.

**Q:** Doesn't retained equity mean a partner I didn't want?
**A:** It can. Define the rights up front — advisory versus control is the distinction that
matters, and it needs to be in writing.
