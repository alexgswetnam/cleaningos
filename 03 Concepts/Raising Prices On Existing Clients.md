# Raising Prices On Existing Clients

> [!abstract] In one line
> Know your number, name it plainly, and be willing to lose the client who won't pay it.

## Definition

The conversation and mindset for moving an existing client — often one you discounted
early on — up to a price that actually makes sense for you, without apologizing for it.

## Why It Matters

Early clients often got a heavily discounted rate to get the business off the ground.
That rate doesn't stop being wrong just because it's familiar. Owners avoid this
conversation out of fear of losing the client, and stay unprofitable on accounts they've
had for years as a result.

## Symptoms

- You know a specific client is underpriced and haven't said anything
- You're scared to raise a rate because "they've been with us so long"
- Margin keeps shrinking on your oldest, most loyal accounts

## Common Mistakes

- Not knowing your number — the minimum price at which this specific client makes sense
- Softening the ask so much it doesn't actually land as a real price change
- Caving to any counter-offer instead of holding your number
- Changing the price on the clean that just happened instead of the one going forward,
  when the actual issue was the home being dirtier than expected

## Models

**Know your number first.** If the client makes sense at $120 and you're charging $90,
$120 is the new price — not a negotiation starting point.

**Name it plainly, and give them the out:**

> "We love serving you, but we gave you a very heavily discounted rate. As we've grown,
> unfortunately this is what it would have to be. I totally understand if it doesn't work
> out for you. Is there any way we can work this out?"

**Be willing to lose them.** If they push back and their counter is below your number,
hold it:

> "I really hate to hear that. We'd love to keep you."

Not everyone stays. That's the point of having a number in the first place.

**Loop in the cleaner, separately.** Raising the client's price doesn't have to mean
changing the cleaner's pay — sometimes it's pure margin. Either way, ask:

> "We're raising prices on this client. Do you like this client? How do you feel about
> the pay?"

**If the trigger is scope, not just time** — the home was dirtier than expected after the
first clean — don't retroactively charge more for the clean that already happened. Set
the new rate going forward instead.

## Checklist

- [ ] Determine your number for this specific client
- [ ] Deliver the price change plainly, with the "I totally understand if it doesn't work
      out" release valve
- [ ] Hold your number against a counter-offer
- [ ] Talk to the cleaner about the client and the pay, separately from the client
      conversation
- [ ] If scope crept, apply the new rate going forward, not retroactively

## Templates

- "We love serving you, but we gave you a very heavily discounted rate. As we've grown,
  unfortunately this is what it would have to be. I totally understand if it doesn't work
  out for you."
- "I really hate to hear that. We'd love to keep you." (when they threaten to cancel)
- "We're raising prices on this client. How do you feel about the pay?" (to the cleaner)
