# Subcontractor Vs W-2

> [!abstract] In one line
> Subcontract until you've got money saved — W-2 is a real cost jump, not a formality swap.

## Definition

The choice between classifying cleaners as 1099 subcontractors or W-2 employees, and when
to make the switch.

## Why It Matters

The two aren't interchangeable paperwork options — they carry a real cost difference
(payroll taxes, potentially insurance, and in some jurisdictions double taxation) that a
newer business often can't absorb yet. Getting the timing wrong in either direction
creates a problem: too early to W-2 strains cash a business doesn't have; describing
subcontractors as "full-time employees" anywhere public (see [[Automated Hiring Pipeline]]) invites misclassification scrutiny from state workforce commissions and the
IRS.

## Symptoms

- Uncertain whether current cleaners are correctly classified
- Website or marketing language calling subcontractors "employees" or "full-time"
- Considering W-2 without having modeled the added cost

## Common Mistakes

- Going W-2 before there's money saved for the cost difference
- Describing subcontractors as employees in public-facing material
- Treating the choice as purely a paperwork preference rather than a real cost decision

## Models

**Default to subcontracting early.** Alex's recommendation for an early-stage business:
subcontract first, switch to W-2 once there's money saved to absorb the cost difference.

> "You probably want to have some money saved before you go to W-2s."

**The cost isn't just payroll tax.** Depending on jurisdiction, there can be compounding
local costs — in Philadelphia, both the business and the employee get taxed again if the
employee lives in the city. Model the actual delta for your specific location before
assuming W-2 is a modest step up.

**Language matters, not just structure.** Calling subcontractors "full-time employees" on
a website is a misclassification signal independent of how they're actually paid — see
[[Automated Hiring Pipeline]]'s Common Mistakes.

## What An Actual Signed Agreement Looks Like

The Faithful Cleaners' Subcontractor Agreement is a real, currently-used 1099 template —
the first concrete example in this vault of what "subcontracting" actually looks like on
paper, not just as a stated preference. The terms that specifically construct
independent-contractor status:

- **Job acceptance is discretionary.** The cleaner can accept or decline any Job.
- **Methods and sequence are the cleaner's own.** The company doesn't direct *how* the
  work gets done, only the outcome.
- **The cleaner supplies their own labor, tools, and supplies**, except where a specific
  work order states otherwise.
- **Pay is per-Job, not hourly**, and the agreement says so explicitly in multiple places
  — including inside the section that could otherwise read as an hourly guarantee (see
  [[Cleaner Pay Structure]]'s Conflict History for where this gets muddled).
- **No exclusivity.** The cleaner is free to work for others.
- **No guaranteed minimum** of Jobs, hours, or earnings.
- **Termination is at-will** (7 days' notice, either party) or immediate for cause
  (material breach, safety violation, intoxication, data breach, criminal activity) —
  not the indefinite at-will employment relationship a W-2 role would carry, but not
  nothing either.

**The parts that go beyond a simple 1099 classification question** — worth knowing about
even though they're not the classification issue itself:

- A **12-month non-solicitation clause**: the cleaner can't solicit or perform cleaning
  work for a client the company introduced, for 12 months after their last Job, without
  going through the company. Breach carries **liquidated damages of the greater of $2,500
  or 40% of the gross revenue from that client in the first 12 months**, plus injunctive
  relief.
- **Insurance minimums the cleaner must carry**: $1M/$2M General Liability naming the
  company as certificate holder, $300K Auto Liability if driving between jobs, and
  Workers' Comp if the cleaner has their own employees.
- **Background checks required** for anyone the cleaner brings onto a client property,
  disclosed in writing first.

None of this is legal advice, and this page records what the document says, not whether
it's the right structure for a given business. Per Constitution §XI's treatment of the
1:1 Coaching Agreement, a signed legal contract is a source for its *content* only —
never for voice, and never as a substitute for actual legal counsel.

## What You Can And Can't Do With A 1099 Cleaner — Labor 101's Operational Rules

Labor 101 gives the concrete, practical version of the control boundary that the signed
agreement expresses legally. Two lessons ("What You CAN'T Do with 1099 Cleaners," "Can I
Train 1099 Cleaners") lay out the same line the agreement draws, in day-to-day terms:

**Cannot control:** their schedule (can't require specific days/hours, can't force
acceptance of an assigned job, can't require on-call availability); the exact means and
methods of the work (can't mandate a specific product, tool, or step-by-step technique —
only the checklist outcome); appearance/branding (no required uniform, no required
branded vehicle); their tools and supplies (contractors provide their own); training in
the employee sense (outcomes and recommendations are fine, mandatory technique is not);
exclusivity (can't ban them from working for other companies or clients); pay mechanics
(can't pay hourly — outcome/job pay only); or discipline (formal write-ups are an
employee-relationship tool, not a 1099 one — the equivalent here is simply not assigning
more jobs).

**Can do:** define the scope of work, the outcome needed, the price for the job, the
deadline, quality expectations via checklist, and audit/inspect the *result*, not the
process used to get there. The plain-language version of the whole boundary: *"You
basically just can't micromanage their exact techniques of what they're doing."*

**Cannot train, can hire trained.** A separate, related lesson makes the hiring-side
implication explicit: because you cannot train a 1099 cleaner the way you'd train an
employee, the business has to hire cleaners who already know how to clean professionally,
then manage them to an outcome — not take someone inexperienced and build their skill
from scratch. Recommending a product or method is fine; requiring one crosses into
employee territory.

## The Operational Case For 1099 (And When A Hybrid Might Make Sense)

A third Labor 101 lesson ("Pros and Cons: 1099 vs W2") gives the fuller operational
argument for starting 1099, consistent with this page's existing "default to
subcontracting early" model, with real detail behind it:

**1099 pros:** faster to start (no payroll system to build), pays for the outcome instead
of the hour (a slow cleaner can't stretch a 6-hour job into 8 and get paid more for it),
and ending the relationship is simple — stop assigning jobs, no termination process.

**1099 cons:** less enforcement power on quality, because you can't train — which is why
hiring already-competent cleaners matters more under this model; an established
contractor with their own other clients may deprioritize your jobs during a busy stretch.

**W-2 pros:** full control over schedule, process, training, standards, uniforms, and
routing; more predictable coverage since employees are looking to the business for income.
**W-2 cons:** payroll tax, workers' comp, admin cost, onboarding pay, and the business is
on the hook for a slow season's wages regardless of booked work — precisely the cash risk
this page's existing "money saved before W-2" guidance is protecting against.

**The named failure mode:** *"Most owners, they want the advantages of a contractor, but
then they operate — they treat those contractors like employees."* That's the compliance
trap, independent of company size.

**Hybrid at scale, stated as a real plan, not just a hypothetical.** The lesson names an
actual threshold: a core W-2 team plus 1099 overflow capacity "if we get to 100K a month
before selling." Below that, for most owners in this course's audience, 1099 alone is the
stated recommendation. This doesn't change this page's existing default-to-1099-early
model — it adds the specific point at which the model's author says he'd reconsider it.

## Helping Cleaners With Their Own 1099 Status

A fourth lesson ("1099 Help for Cleaners") is cleaner-facing rather than owner-facing —
what a cleaner needs to know about handling their own taxes as an independent contractor
(W-9, quarterly estimated payments, tracking income/expenses, the 1099-NEC at year end,
tools like Keeper and Bonsai Tax). It doesn't change the classification decision this
page covers, but it's worth noting here: consistently treating cleaners as genuinely
self-directed businesses — including helping them understand their own tax obligations
rather than treating that as the company's problem — is itself part of what keeps the
classification real rather than nominal.
