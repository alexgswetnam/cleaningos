# Add-Backs And SDE

> [!abstract] In one line
> SDE is the seller's argument, not a fact. Read the add-backs and decide how much of it you believe.

## Definition

**SDE** — Seller's Discretionary Earnings. Profit, plus everything the seller argues a new
owner wouldn't have to spend. It's the number a business is priced on.

**Add-backs** — the individual items being added back. Depreciation, the owner's salary,
one-off costs, and personal expenses that were run through the business.

Every add-back is a claim: *you won't have to pay this.* Some are true.

## Why It Matters

The purchase price is a multiple of SDE, so every dollar added back is multiplied. At 2–2.2x
(see [[Business Valuation]]), a $10,000 add-back that shouldn't be there costs a buyer
$20,000–$22,000.

The seller assembles this number. Nobody is obliged to make it conservative.

## Symptoms

- Add-backs change sharply year to year with no explanation
- You're given line summaries and not transactions
- The books are pen-and-paper, and figures were "assembled" for the sale
- The financials cover the two best years and stop

## Common Mistakes

- Accepting line summaries. Ask for transactions.
- Not asking why add-backs changed between years
- Accepting the most recent two years and not asking for the current one
- Forgetting the biggest add-back is often the one nobody listed — the owner's unpaid labor.
  See [[Owner-Dependent Revenue]].
- Treating messy books as automatically disqualifying, or automatically an opportunity.
  Neither. They mean you don't know yet.

## Models

**The three questions, in order:**

1. *"Are we going to be able to see basically every single transaction in the business, or
   are we just going to see line summaries?"*
2. Why did the add-backs change between years?
3. What's missing from this that a new owner would actually have to pay?

**The worked example.** Add-backs roughly doubled between 2023 and 2024. Pressed, the broker
explained it as:

| Item | Type |
|---|---|
| A depreciation line | Legitimate — non-cash |
| Self-insurance | Personal expense run through the business |
| Life insurance | Personal expense run through the business |
| A personal "mountain house" — no business use, no equipment stored there | Personal expense run through the business |

None of that is unusual and none of it is fraud. It's normal small-business behaviour, and
it's exactly why the headline number needs unpicking rather than trusting.

**Find a source of truth outside the seller's spreadsheet.** Here that's the tax filings —
the one set of numbers prepared for an audience with no incentive to flatter.

> The books "aren't a clean source of truth." The owner is "missing some pretty big stuff."

**On messy bookkeeping as a buying signal.** One of the buyers noted a book listing bad
bookkeeping as a *positive* on a good-deal checklist — the theory being you can come in and
clean it up and unlock value. They stayed sceptical, and the scepticism is the right
position: messy books might hide upside or might hide a problem, and the only way to know is
to do the work.

## Checklist

- [ ] Request transaction-level detail, not summaries
- [ ] Get three years, including the current one — not just the two best
- [ ] Itemise every add-back and classify it: non-cash, genuinely discretionary, or personal
- [ ] Ask why the total moved between years
- [ ] Add back the *missing* item: unpaid owner labor
- [ ] Reconcile against tax filings
- [ ] Have a CPA review before the due diligence window closes — see [[Due Diligence]]

## FAQ

**Q:** Are personal expenses in the books a red flag?
**A:** Not by themselves — it's ordinary. It becomes a problem when it's undisclosed, or when
you can't tell which expenses were genuinely discretionary.

**Q:** What if the seller won't give transaction detail?
**A:** That's information. Price accordingly or walk.
