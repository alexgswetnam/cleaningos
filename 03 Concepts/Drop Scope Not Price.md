# Drop Scope Not Price

> [!abstract] In one line
> Meet their budget by cleaning less, never by charging less per hour.

## Definition

When a prospect genuinely can't afford the quote, you reduce what's included rather than
reducing your rate. Their total comes down. Your rate per hour doesn't move.

## Why It Matters

A discount permanently resets what that client expects to pay, and it drags your average
ticket down — one of the four numbers in [[Marketing Math]]. Reduced scope keeps the rate
intact while still solving their problem.

It also keeps the conversation honest. You're not pretending the work is worth less; you
are doing less work.

## Symptoms

- "That's more than I was hoping to spend"
- You're about to knock $40 off to save the booking
- Your effective hourly rate varies wildly by client

## Common Mistakes

- Cutting the rate to win the job
- Cutting scope without saying what's being cut — leads to disappointment and a bad review
- Confusing this with [[Not Every Client Is Your Client]]: if the gap is a whole league,
  reduced scope won't bridge it either

## Models

> "We can focus on bathrooms, kitchen, and high-touch areas. We skip the unused rooms.
> That brings it to a price that works for you."

Cut the rooms nobody uses. Keep the rooms that make the house feel clean.

**The [[Objection Handling Course]] version of the same opening question** — a second,
independent source for this exact technique:

> "Are there any rooms in the home, any areas in the home that are unused or that we don't
> really need to clean because they're just not that active?"

Course's example: a 3,000 sqft home with two empty nesters who only actively use the
bedroom, living room, kitchen, and one bathroom. Ask before you assume — let the reduction
follow from their answer, not from a guess at what they don't use.

**The only discount that exists:** $10–15 off the first clean in exchange for a review.
Small, rare, and used at the finish line — when someone is about to cross and wants to
feel like they got something.

> [!warning] This line is contested — see Conflict History below.

**Two more shapes the reduction can take.** Both hold the rate and both let the prospect
help build the solution:

**Cap the time.**

> "Why don't we cap the time at three hours for this price? We'll focus on kitchens and
> bathrooms — the high-touch areas — and do the rest as we're able during that time."

**Rotate the rooms.**

> "What if we did the basement every other week instead of every week? One week vacuum the
> living room, next week the basement. That keeps it manageable and keeps your price where
> you need it."

**Let them co-create it.** Prospects hand you the cuts if you let them — *"I don't really
use my basement," "if we could do it every other week."* Those aren't objections; they're
the prospect designing their own package. A plan they helped build is one they feel
ownership of, and it stops being a discount at all.

**Scope-negotiating is a buying signal.** Someone rearranging what's included has already
decided they want the service. See [[Sales Happen On The Phone]] — that's the moment not to
end the call.

**A second call gives the same "handle the heavy stuff, they take the easier parts" shape,
independently, as the default answer to a budget objection** — worth noting because it
matches Claim B's style (a discretionary, in-the-moment restructuring) more than Claim A's
"$10–15 off for a review" ceiling:

> "Something we do for people is we handle the heavy lifting — bathrooms, appliances, the
> harder stuff. And if you want to take care of the vacuuming and the easier parts, we have
> a more affordable option."

This turns a $0 option (the prospect cleans it themselves) into a $150–$200 booking — not
a discount on the full price, a smaller job at the full rate.

**Rick's live win, same call, is the rotate-the-rooms move worked in real time.** A
bi-weekly prospect's price "seemed high." Rick asked whether she needed every room every
time; she didn't. He built: deep clean first, then alternate — main floor only one week,
whole house the next. Her response: *"This is exactly what I was looking for."* Booked on
the first call. Same mechanism as the "rotate the rooms" shape already on this page, now
with a second worked example and no reciprocity language attached — the rate held, the
scope changed, nothing was framed as a favor.

## Checklist

- [ ] Confirm it's a real budget constraint, not a league gap — *"is it just a price thing?"*
- [ ] Get buy-in first: *"if I find a fair price, would you book right now?"*
- [ ] Ask what they don't use. Let them make the cut.
- [ ] Name exactly which areas you'll cover
- [ ] Name exactly what you're skipping — out loud, on the call
- [ ] Hold the rate
- [ ] Document the reduced scope so the cleaner doesn't over-deliver into a loss

## Student Examples

**Marcella** (Jack's call) — offered her own cuts unprompted: *"I don't really use my
basement," "if we could do it every other week."* The prescribed response was to cap the time
at three hours and focus on kitchens and bathrooms. Jack instead came back later with a
lower flat number and lost the booking.

**Rick's bi-weekly prospect, April 2** — named her own budget ceiling by tone ("price is
going to seem high"). Asked whether she needed every room every time, got a no, built an
alternating deep-clean/main-floor plan. Booked on the spot, no reciprocity framing, no
discount stated anywhere in the call.

## FAQ

**Q:** Doesn't this just look like a discount to them?
**A:** No — they can see what they're not getting. That's the difference. A discount says
the work was overpriced; reduced scope says less work costs less.
