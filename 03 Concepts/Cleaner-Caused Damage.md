# Cleaner-Caused Damage

> [!abstract] In one line
> Never take blame before you've actually investigated — but still make the client whole,
> because the subcontractor's insurance and agreement determine who *pays*, not whether
> the client gets taken care of.

## Definition

The process for handling property damage caused during a clean: gathering facts before
assigning fault, deciding who bears the cost (the cleaner's own insurance, the business's
insurance, or the business absorbing it directly) based on the subcontractor agreement,
and making the client whole regardless of which of those turns out to be true. Distinct
from [[Quality Complaints]] (a *quality* dispute — missed or incomplete work) — this is
specifically physical damage to a client's property.

## Why It Matters

Damage is rare but real, and how it's handled affects both the client relationship and
the business's actual financial exposure. Getting the sequence wrong in either direction
is costly: admitting fault before the facts are known can create liability the business
doesn't actually have, while failing to make a legitimately affected client whole damages
trust and the brand's satisfaction-guarantee promise. Because cleaners are 1099
subcontractors, a well-structured subcontractor agreement and verified cleaner insurance
are what determine whether a given incident is the business's cost or the cleaner's
business's cost — see [[Subcontractor Vs W-2]].

## Symptoms

- A client reports something broken or damaged during or after a clean
- Uncertainty about whether the business, the cleaner, or insurance should cover a repair
- No proof of insurance on file for a cleaner before they're assigned jobs
- A cleaner denies responsibility for something the evidence suggests they caused

## Common Mistakes

- Admitting fault ("we messed up," "that was our fault") before investigating
- Not collecting proof of insurance from cleaners before assigning them any work
- Letting a cleaner who lied about causing damage continue working
- Treating "we're not liable per the subcontractor agreement" as the end of the
  conversation with the client, instead of still working to make them whole
- Having no reserve fund set aside for the rare cases the business does end up covering

## Models

**Step one, always: listen and gather facts — don't assign blame yet.** Get the client's
full account, ask for photos, get the cleaner's explanation, and if needed bring in a
specialist for an independent assessment before deciding anything. The specific language
to use with a client while investigating deliberately avoids admitting or denying fault:
*"I can see how that was possible... I understand your perspective... I can't promise
anything right now at this moment. I just need to work through the situation together."*

**Who pays traces back to the subcontractor agreement and insurance status, not
sympathy.** A cleaner operating as a genuine 1099 business is contractually liable for
damage their business causes — *"it's his cleaner, it's his business, he's liable."* If
the cleaner (or their own business, if they run a team) has proof of insurance on file,
that insurance is the intended first resort. Absent that — for instance, with a newer
cleaner whose insurance wasn't yet collected — the business may end up paying out of
pocket, which is a documented, real gap this business has hit and corrected going
forward.

**Make the client whole regardless of who ultimately pays.** Even in a case where the
business isn't contractually liable, the stated priority is still to fix the client's
problem: *"My one job is to make sure that you're satisfied and work through this
properly with you... our satisfaction guarantee isn't just some words we're throwing out
there."* Whether that repair cost is later recovered from the responsible subcontractor is
a separate question from whether the client gets taken care of now.

**When a cleaner lies about what happened, that's a bigger problem than the damage
itself.** In a documented case, a cleaner scratched shower glass with pumice-stone debris
on her cloth and then told both the client and the business the damage pre-existed. The
business didn't accept either side's account at face value — a glass specialist was
brought in, quotes were gathered independently, and the physical evidence settled it. The
dishonesty, not just the damage, is what determines whether that cleaner continues working
at all.

**Insurance basics, kept simple.** Every cleaner should carry their own general liability
insurance (recommended providers: Thimble, Next Insurance — roughly $30–50/month) on file
before being assigned jobs. It's rarely actually used, but it's what protects the business
from being the only backstop when a real, costly mistake happens (the example given: a
vacuum's roller bar damaging a $30,000 rug). If a candidate is struggling to afford basic
insurance, that itself is worth factoring into the hiring decision.

**Keep a reserve fund.** Setting aside profit specifically for unexpected costs like this
(a "rainy day fund") is what makes the out-of-pocket cases — the ones insurance doesn't
end up covering — survivable without disrupting cash flow.

## Checklist

- [ ] Collect proof of insurance from every cleaner before assigning any job — no
      exceptions
- [ ] When damage is reported, gather facts and photos before assigning blame
- [ ] Use non-committal, empathetic language while investigating — don't admit or deny
      fault early
- [ ] Bring in an independent specialist/quote when the facts are disputed
- [ ] Determine liability from the subcontractor agreement and insurance status, not
      sympathy or convenience
- [ ] Make the client whole regardless of who ultimately bears the cost
- [ ] If a cleaner is found to have lied about causing damage, treat that as a standalone,
      serious issue for their continued employment
- [ ] Maintain a reserve fund for the cases the business ends up covering directly

## Student Examples

**The source company, four real incidents over roughly a year at six figures of
revenue:** a vase knocked over (no cost — an understanding, previously-relationship-built
client); a curtain rod pulled from drywall (the assigned subcontractor's own team member
fixed it directly, no cost to the business); bleach discoloration on bathroom fixtures
($500 fix, paid by the business directly because the responsible cleaner's insurance
wasn't on file at the time — a documented process gap, since corrected); and a scratched
shower-glass panel from pumice-stone debris, where the cleaner lied about causing it —
resolved via independent glass-specialist quotes (initially feared at $3,000–5,000,
actually ~$1,100), covered by the responsible subcontractor's own liability per the
agreement, not the business.

## FAQ

**Q:** If the subcontractor agreement says the cleaner is liable, does the business have
any obligation to the client at all?
**A:** Legally, per the agreement, no — but the source is explicit that the business
still prioritizes making the client whole as a matter of the satisfaction-guarantee
promise and professionalism, separate from who ultimately covers the cost.
