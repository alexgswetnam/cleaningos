# Testing A New Cleaner

> [!abstract] In one line
> Partner them with your best cleaner for two or three cleans, then ask your best cleaner.

## Definition

Before a new cleaner touches a paying client from marketing, they run a paid test clean —
or two or three — partnered with an existing, established cleaner, on a small, low-risk
job. It's framed to the candidate as a slow on-ramp, not a promise of ongoing work. Your
best cleaners give the verdict, alongside client feedback.

## Why It Matters

It is the answer to the fear that blocks hiring. You are not gambling your reviews on
someone unproven — you are testing them behind your best people first. See
[[Reviews As Asset Not Identity]]. An interview can mislead: someone can look experienced
and personable on paper and in conversation, and the business still doesn't actually know
whether they're a fit until they're inside a real client's home.

## Symptoms

- Sending a newly-hired cleaner solo to a client before ever observing their work
- No feedback loop from either the client or an established cleaner after a first job
- Assigning a brand-new cleaner to a move-out, a first-time deep clean, or a difficult
  client as their first assignment
- Treating the interview alone as sufficient evidence of fit

## Common Mistakes

- Turning on marketing before the test is complete
- Sending a new cleaner solo to a new client
- Skipping your existing cleaners' opinion
- Testing on a high-risk job type: move-outs, first-time deep cleans, extreme clutter, or
  a client already known to be difficult
- Expecting day-one perfection instead of evaluating for coachability and a trend of
  improvement
- Enabling direct client-cleaner communication during the test out of convenience — see
  [[Should Clients Have A Cleaner's Number]]

## Models

**The test is paid, but framed as a slow on-ramp.** Tell the candidate plainly up front:
this isn't "you're in for the next year," it's a controlled evaluation of fit on both
sides. No scorecard is required — pairing with an established cleaner and asking for
their read afterward is enough.

**Choose a small, low-risk job for the test.** Not a move-out, not a first-time deep
clean, not a known-difficult client, not extreme clutter. If a reclean is needed because
the test didn't go well, it should be cheap to fix. A slower-than-expected pace on the
first job or two is acceptable as long as the quality is there — new cleaners often need
a warm-up period.

**What the established cleaner and the business are actually evaluating:**
professionalism and client-facing conduct (not on speakerphone, no bad odor complaints,
warm and communicative with the client); pace (not rushing and missing things, but not
unreasonably slow either); quality (details — corners, edges, baseboards, faucets,
streaks); no cross-contamination (different rags for different surfaces); communication
(asking the client questions rather than guessing); and whether they do a real final
walkthrough with the client, inviting feedback before leaving.

**Outcome-based checklists, not step-by-step training — this is a 1099 boundary, not just
a testing technique.** The checklist tells a cleaner *what* needs to be true when they're
done (the floor is mopped, the corners are hit, nothing's on the floor) — never the exact
product, tool, or sequence to use. That distinction matters beyond quality control: it's
also what keeps a 1099 relationship from drifting into employee-style control. See
[[Subcontractor Vs W-2]].

**Both cleaners check each other's work before the final client walkthrough.** However the
job gets split (upstairs/downstairs, room by room, or working the same rooms together),
the established cleaner and the test cleaner cross-check before anyone declares the job
done and walks the client through it.

**Pay the new (support) cleaner differently than the established (lead) cleaner** on a
paired job — this is a distinct decision from the base [[Cleaner Pay Structure]] split,
reflecting that the newer cleaner is doing less of the client-facing and quality-assurance
work.

**Missed items get fixed, not paid twice.** If the test cleaner misses something the
checklist required, the fix is on them — this mirrors the same-course rule already on
[[Quality Complaints]]: paid for the completed outcome, not for showing up.

**The three-strike system tracks ongoing performance once testing is over** — see
[[Three-Strike System]] for how complaints and re-cleans get logged and turned into a
keep/reassign/remove decision over time, distinct from the initial pass/fail test itself.

## Checklist

- [ ] Frame the test explicitly as a paid slow on-ramp, not a guarantee
- [ ] Choose a small, low-risk job — never a move-out, first deep clean, or known-difficult
      client
- [ ] Pair with an established cleaner for the first test clean(s)
- [ ] Give an outcome-based checklist, not step-by-step technique instructions
- [ ] Both cleaners check each other's work before the client walkthrough
- [ ] Established cleaner does a final walkthrough with the client, inviting feedback
- [ ] Call the new cleaner afterward for their side
- [ ] Call the established cleaner afterward for their read on fit
- [ ] Check with the client directly on how it went
- [ ] Decide: continue the on-ramp, place them in easier/support roles only, or don't
      continue
- [ ] Pay the support cleaner differently than the lead cleaner on paired jobs

## Student Examples

**Courtney** — Alex's instruction: *"partner him up and give your best girls' opinion on
that."* Two or three cleans, then confirm.

**Labor Basics coaching call.** A cleaner (Erica) proved strong on recurring standard
cleans over ten months but was identified early, through testing and placement, as weak
on deep cleans and move-outs — so she was scheduled almost exclusively onto the same
recurring clients rather than pushed into job types she wasn't suited for. The lesson
explicitly frames this as a placement decision, not a firing decision: a "B-level" cleaner
doesn't have to get "A-level" jobs to still be valuable.

## FAQ

**Q:** Do you have to send a new cleaner with a partner every single time, or can they be
told about it in advance?
**A:** The source doesn't flag it either way — pairing without telling the client is the
default, except for clients who specifically expect their usual assigned cleaner, where
advance notice matters more.
