# Word Tracks

> [!abstract] In one line
> Knowing your exact line frees you to actually listen, instead of scrambling for what to say next.

## Definition

A word track is the exact sentence you say when a specific objection comes up. Not a
general approach — the literal words, known well enough that you don't have to assemble
them on the call.

## Why It Matters

> "There's a difference between me trying to put the words together versus I know that
> this is what I'm going to say."

When you're building a response mid-sentence, all your attention is on you. You're
thinking *what am I going to say, what am I going to say.* You can't hear the person.

When the line is automatic, attention goes back to them. You hear their tone, their
personality, what they're actually worried about — and you adjust the delivery of a line
you already know.

**The word track doesn't make you robotic. It's what makes you present.**

## Symptoms

- You feel anxious going into the objection part of the call
- You "feel out" each person and improvise
- You can't say what you'd reply to "let me think about it" without thinking first
- Deals die at the same moment every time and you can't say why

## Common Mistakes

- Improvising every objection and calling it "being natural"
- Having an approach but not a sentence
- Learning the words but not rehearsing them enough to stop thinking about them

## Models

Same words, adjusted delivery, per personality — the delivery half is [[Mirroring]]:

| Person | Delivery |
|---|---|
| Type A, wants the point | Direct, quick. *"Absolutely. What's the main concern you need to think about?"* |
| Chatty, warm | Softer. *"Absolutely, no worries. Of course you want to think about it. I don't want you to feel any pressure at all. I'm just curious — what's the main concern you need to think about?"* |

**A generic three-touch follow-up track**, for a quoted lead who's gone quiet — distinct
from the objection-specific tracks above, and complementary to [[Sales Pipeline Stages]]'s Quoted-stage cadence (follow up every 1–2 days): a light check-in at 24
hours, a scarcity-framed nudge at 48 hours, and a door-left-open final message rather
than one more push.

**A reusable framework for building a word track from scratch: Acknowledge → Clarify →
Solve.** For any objection not yet in your library — write down the objections you hear
weekly (too expensive, spouse, thinking about it, other quotes, cheaper, availability,
trustworthiness), then build one response per objection using this shape:

1. **Acknowledge** — *"Yes, that makes sense — I get that."*
2. **Clarify** — isolate the real blocker: *"Is it just the price that's keeping you
   from booking?"*
3. **Solve** — get the conditional commitment: *"So if you were happy with the price,
   you would book a cleaning with us right now?"*

Once they agree, any concession offered next (a small one-time discount tied to leaving
a review, dropping an add-on, a recurring-only discount) is closed with a single line —
*"Sound fair enough?"* — and the moment they say yes, move straight to the card, using
whatever preferred time was already banked earlier in the call. See
[[Drop Scope Not Price]] for the scope-reduction version of the same concession
principle, and [[Speed To Lead]] for the "ask timing before price" technique that sets
this up.

**A second source restates Acknowledge → Clarify → Solve as a general rule for any
objection, not just the named ones this page already links out to.** Framed as "agree
first, then probe deeper": validate what the prospect said (*"Yeah, that makes total
sense"*), then ask what they mean specifically rather than assuming the first sentence is
the whole objection (*"What is it that you need to think about specifically?"*), then —
only once the real concern surfaces — offer a solution that fits it. The analogy used:
*"You're kind of like the cleaning doctor"* — diagnose before you prescribe, the same
metaphor [[Diagnose Don't Take Orders]] uses for the opening of the call, now applied to
the objection moment instead of the intake question. This doesn't add a new step to the
existing framework; it confirms the framework already on this page is the general pattern,
not a technique specific to any one named objection.

**Training the word track — how it goes from written to automatic.** A companion lesson
answers the question this page's checklist assumes but doesn't spell out: how do you
actually get a word track to the point where you don't have to think about it? The answer
is repetition, not exposure — practice out loud, roleplay with a partner, record attempts
and listen back. The specific thing to listen for: lines that come out "mumbly jumbly" —
too many words, awkward in the mouth — should be simplified, not memorized as written. A
worked example: a long, hedged question about the home's condition simplifies to a
one-to-ten scale plus one follow-up (*"What makes you say that?"*). Beyond the words
themselves, train tonality, pace, and talking less rather than more — the goal is knowing
the outline and the key lines well enough to stop looking at the script, not reciting it.
**Confidence is named as the single most important piece of this training**, ahead of
exact wording.

**Confidence changes how the identical words land — a distinct claim from the delivery
material already on this page.** Where [[Price Delivery]] and [[Mirroring]] cover *how*
to say a specific line, this source makes the broader claim that general uncertainty in
speech — "maybe," "I think," and upward inflection on statements that should read as
fact — communicates uncertainty about the *business*, not just the price. *"If you sound
unsure, the customer feels unsure about your ability to help them."* The fix isn't a
delivery trick: it's building real belief that the cleaners do good work, the guarantee is
real, and the client will be taken care of — then confirming it by listening back to
calls. Fake confidence without that belief doesn't hold.

## Checklist

- [ ] Write the exact sentence for each objection you lose to
- [ ] Say it out loud until you don't have to think about it
- [ ] Run a call and notice whether you can hear them better
- [ ] For a new objection: acknowledge, clarify the real blocker, then solve for a
      conditional yes before offering any concession
- [ ] Training a new word track: roleplay it, record it, and simplify any line that comes
      out "mumbly jumbly" rather than memorizing it as originally written
- [ ] Before blaming a specific word track, check for general uncertainty in speech —
      "maybe," "I think," upward inflection on factual statements

## FAQ

**Q:** Doesn't a script make me sound fake?
**A:** The opposite. Scrambling for words is what sounds fake — you're visibly not
listening. The line is what buys you the attention to be present.
