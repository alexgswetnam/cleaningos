# Handling Let Me Talk To My Spouse

> [!abstract] In one line
> The spouse is rarely the real objection — validate, then ask what their spouse's concern would be, and you'll usually hear the prospect's own concern instead.

## Definition

The response to "let me talk to my husband/wife/roommate" before booking. A single
validating line followed by one question that surfaces the real objection hiding behind
the decision-maker excuse — the same shape as [[Handling Let Me Think About It]], applied
to a different stall.

## Why It Matters

Most of the time the spouse doesn't have a concern the prospect can't see. The person on
the phone has a concern themselves and doesn't want to say it directly, so they borrow
someone else's authority to exit the call. Salespeople who accept "I'll talk to them and
get back to you" at face value are accepting a soft no with no next step — the lead
usually ghosts.

## Symptoms

- "Let me talk to my husband/wife/roommate about it and get back to you"
- You say "sure, no problem" and never hear back
- The prospect who "needs to check" never actually mentions your call to anyone
- You can't tell whether a booking died on price, timing, or the spouse

## Common Mistakes

- Accepting "I'll talk to them and get back to you" as a real next step, with no
  commitment attached
- Asking the follow-up question in a confrontational or sharp tone — it reads as an
  interrogation instead of a normal conversation
- Skipping validation and jumping straight to a challenging question, which makes their
  walls go up
- Asking a yes/no question when you need to uncover the concern
- Asking "Does that work for you?" when you could assume the close and ask for the next
  booking detail instead
- Leaving the call with no commitment when the spouse is already confirmed to be on board

## Models

**Validate first — do not jump straight to the question:**

> "Got it, that totally makes sense. No worries at all. What do you think would be your
> husband's main concern here?"

Tone carries this line. Smile while you say it. *"What's your wife's concern here?"* asked
flatly sounds like an interrogation. *"Got it, yeah, that totally makes sense, man. If you
don't mind me asking, what is your wife's main concern here?"* asked warm sounds like a
normal conversation. Same words underneath, different result.

This question usually surfaces the actual objection — often price, sometimes timing or
trust. Once it's named, handle that objection directly (see [[Price Objection]] if it's
price).

**If they genuinely do need spousal buy-in** — a shared account, an agreement about
purchases — check whether the spouse is already on board with the idea of cleaning at all,
before assuming you need to sell the spouse too:

> "Yeah, that makes sense. Is your wife on board with the house cleaning or is this
> something that you were just personally looking into here?"

Most of the time the answer is yes. Once it is, move to a soft commitment rather than
waiting for a second phone call that likely never happens:

> "Got it. Okay. Well, I don't normally do this, but here's what I can do for you. We can
> get this appointment booked like we talked about for Monday at 3 p.m. And if for any
> reason your wife does object, something doesn't work for her and she wants to cancel,
> then we can go ahead and get that canceled for you free of charge. We'll completely
> waive that cancellation fee. What's a good email address for you?"

**Assume the close.** Ask for the next booking detail — not "does that work for you?" If
it doesn't work, they'll say so, and that's another chance to address the real concern
rather than a dead end.

## Checklist

- [ ] They say they need to talk to a spouse/roommate/decision maker
- [ ] Validate: "that totally makes sense, no worries at all" — smile while saying it
- [ ] Ask what their spouse's main concern would be
- [ ] Listen for the real objection underneath — usually price
- [ ] Address that concern directly
- [ ] If spousal approval is genuinely required, ask whether the spouse is already on
      board with the idea of cleaning
- [ ] If yes, offer to book now with a free-cancellation safety net if the spouse objects
- [ ] Assume the close — ask for the next booking detail, not "does that work for you?"
- [ ] If they still won't book, send a quote and follow up — most people won't act on a
      quote alone
- [ ] If they later cancel citing the spouse, call and ask what specifically didn't work

## FAQ

**Q:** What if they really do need to talk to their spouse — this isn't just a stall?
**A:** Ask whether the spouse is already on board with cleaning generally. If yes, book
with a free-cancellation option rather than waiting for a second call that may never
happen. If no, you may genuinely need to wait — but get a specific date to follow up on
rather than an open-ended "I'll get back to you."
