# Due Diligence

> [!abstract] In one line
> The window after the LOI where you can still walk. Use all of it, and go stand on the job site.

## Definition

The verification period following a Letter of Intent, during which a buyer confirms
everything they've been told and retains the right to back out.

The LOI is not the purchase. It's the agreement to look properly.

## Why It Matters

This is [[Five Laws|Stop Guessing]] with money attached, and it's the only stage where
disbelief is free. Every question you don't ask here becomes a thing you own.

The broker call is a demonstration of why: the single most consequential fact about that
business — that the owner performs 15–20 unpaid hours a week on a quarter of the revenue —
was not in the materials. It surfaced because somebody kept asking. See
[[Owner-Dependent Revenue]].

## Symptoms

- You're relying on a summary document to describe a business
- You've talked to a broker and not to the owner
- You've read financials and never seen the work happen
- You feel committed before you've verified anything

## Common Mistakes

- Treating the LOI as the finish line
- Verifying on paper only — books can be right and the business still wrong
- Not using a CPA
- Being polite. The broker didn't volunteer the owner's labor hours; it came out under
  rapid-fire questioning.
- Rushing the window because you want the deal

## Models

**Two kinds of verification, and you need both:**

| Paper | In person |
|---|---|
| CPA review of the financials | Observe the owner run a job site, day to day |
| Transactions, not summaries — see [[Add-Backs And SDE]] | Meet the employees |
| Reconcile against tax filings | See the equipment working |
| Client list with revenue per client — see [[Client Concentration]] | Watch what the owner actually does all day |

The right-hand column is what catches owner dependency. Nothing on paper says *the owner is
the only one who can do this.*

**The rapid-fire method.** The buyers open by naming what they're about to do —

> "I'm just going to rapid fire if it's cool with everyone."

— then work through financials, assets, dependency, concentration, and contracts. Announcing
the format gives permission for questions that would otherwise seem aggressive one at a
time.

**Ask for the current year.** Two years of financials is what gets offered. The buyers wanted
2025 as well — the year in progress, which is the one nobody has had time to arrange.

## Checklist

- [ ] Sign the NDA, request the CIM
- [ ] Rapid-fire the broker: financials, add-backs, assets, dependency, concentration, contracts
- [ ] Sign an LOI with a defined due diligence window and a right to withdraw
- [ ] CPA review of transaction-level financials
- [ ] Reconcile to tax filings
- [ ] Request the current year, not just the last two complete ones
- [ ] Service records on vehicles and equipment; confirm nothing idle is idle because it's broken
- [ ] Spend a day on site watching the owner work
- [ ] Client list with revenue per client and contract status
- [ ] Agree the [[Non-Compete]] and transition period
- [ ] Only then, structure the deal — see [[Deal Structure]]

## FAQ

**Q:** How long should the due diligence window be?
**A:** **Not specified in any ingested source.** Negotiable, and it should be long enough for
a CPA review plus time on site.

**Q:** Do I need a CPA if the books look simple?
**A:** Simple books are the case for one. In the source the books were pen-and-paper and the
biggest item was missing entirely.
