# Ideal Customer Profile

> [!abstract] In one line
> Start with pet owners, busy professionals, and retired/elderly clients — then ask on every call why they're actually looking, and tailor your words to that.

## Definition

A starting definition of who the business is trying to attract, used to tailor marketing
copy, ad targeting, and sales-call language to what that specific type of person cares
about. Distinct from [[Not Every Client Is Your Client]], which is the decision to walk
away from a lead once red flags appear — this page is the earlier decision of who to aim
at in the first place.

## Why It Matters

Marketing written for "everyone" speaks to no one. A pet owner, a busy professional, and a
retired homeowner are worried about different things and respond to different language.
Knowing the ICP changes what an ad says, what a sales call asks, and who the business
should be trying to reach at all — including which prospects to *not* chase, since some
customer types (the cheapest, most price-focused segment) are a poor fit for a full-price
service model regardless of how many of them exist.

## Symptoms

- Ads and sales scripts use the same generic language for every prospect
- Attracting mostly price-focused leads despite a premium positioning
- Not asking prospects why they're actually looking for a cleaner
- Treating "big house" or "has pets" as an automatic proxy for a good client

## Common Mistakes

- Marketing to everyone instead of a defined starting archetype
- Assuming every pet owner, retired person, or wealthy homeowner is automatically a fit
- Not customizing the offer for archetypes with different needs (e.g. elderly clients who
  don't use every room)
- Copying a competitor's ICP language without checking whether it matches your own
  business identity

## Models

**Three starting archetypes.** Pet owners, busy professionals, and retired/elderly people
who travel, have money, and don't want to clean themselves. Not the only possible ICP, but
a strong, tested starting point before building anything more elaborate.

**Ask why on every call.** The single most useful sales-call question for ICP purposes:
why are you looking for a cleaning service? Pets, kids, a busy schedule, or a home that's
become a hazard situation all point to a different underlying desire — and the desire is
what should be sold to, not the checklist of tasks.

**Not every instance of an archetype is a good client.** Being a pet owner doesn't
automatically make someone a fit — a pet-overrun home on a rock-bottom budget is a
different prospect than a busy professional pet owner who wants reliable, high-quality
service. The archetype is a starting filter, not a guarantee.

**Elderly clients often need a customized plan, not the standard package.** Many are on a
fixed budget (social security or a set monthly amount) and may not use every room in the
house. The right move is often a smaller, tailored clean rather than the full standard
service — treat this as a service-design decision, not a discount.

**Busy professionals are called out as a favorite client type.** A traveling nurse,
doctor, or other high earner understands their own time is worth more than the time it
would take to clean themselves. Speak directly to reliability, communication, trust, and
the risk of hiring someone unprofessional — not to price.

**A big house or visible wealth doesn't guarantee low price-sensitivity.** Some large-home
clients are still frugal and will negotiate hard. The behavioral signal that predicts a
good long-term client isn't home size or apparent wealth — it's whether the person
negotiates, is transparent, and is easy on the phone. Clients who don't ask for a discount
and aren't difficult tend to stay the longest.

**Contrast case: the budget-shopper ICP is a real, different business model.** Homeaglow's
own ideal customer is explicitly the cheapest-possible-price shopper — not a mistake on
their part, just a different ICP than a full-price, white-glove positioning. The lesson
isn't "budget shoppers are bad clients" in the abstract; it's that they're the wrong ICP
for *this* model, and marketing built for a premium ICP will misfire if budget shoppers are
who it actually reaches.

## Checklist

- [ ] Start with pet owners, busy professionals, and retired/elderly clients as the
      default three archetypes
- [ ] On every sales call, ask why the prospect is looking for a cleaning service
- [ ] Write ad and website copy that speaks to a specific archetype's desire, not a
      generic list of services
- [ ] Customize the offer for archetypes with different needs (e.g. elderly clients)
- [ ] Watch behavior, not just demographics — negotiation and tone predict fit better than
      home size or apparent wealth
- [ ] If attracting mostly price shoppers, check whether the marketing itself is speaking
      to the wrong ICP
