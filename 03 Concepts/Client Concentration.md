# Client Concentration

> [!abstract] In one line
> Fifty clients sounds safe until four of them are forty percent of the revenue.

## Definition

How much of the revenue sits with how few clients. A business with the same top line can be
sturdy or fragile depending entirely on this distribution, and the headline client count
hides it.

> "Do we have 50 clients, but four of them make up 40% of the revenue?"

## Why It Matters

Losing one client out of fifty is a rounding error. Losing one client who is 15% of revenue
is a layoff.

For a buyer it's a discount to the price and sometimes a reason to walk. For an owner it's
the thing that quietly caps what the business is worth — you can grow revenue for a year and
make the business more fragile the whole time if the growth is concentrated.

It also applies to *services*, not just clients. If one service line is 25% of revenue and
only one person can deliver it, that's the same risk wearing different clothes — see
[[Owner-Dependent Revenue]].

## Symptoms

- You can name your top three clients without checking
- One client's schedule change moves your month
- A single commercial contract funds your payroll
- You've never actually run the distribution

## Common Mistakes

- Asking for the client count and stopping there
- Assuming recurring means secure — see [[Uncontracted Recurring Revenue]]
- Measuring concentration by client and not by service line
- As an owner, chasing one big account because it's easier than twenty small ones. It is
  easier, and it's how you end up with a business you can't sell.

## Models

**Run the distribution, not the count.** Sort clients by revenue, descending, and ask what
share the top 1, top 5, and top 10 represent. That's the whole analysis.

**Two forms of concentration:**

| Form | Question | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Client | What share of revenue is the top handful? | One departure, one bad month |
| Service / person | Is one line delivered by one person? | That person leaves, the line goes |

**When the seller can't tell you**, that's a finding. In the broker call the numbers weren't
available at all — the owner was pen-and-paper and it had to be assembled by hand. A business
that can't produce its own revenue distribution on request has not been managed with this in
mind.

## Checklist

- [ ] Get the client list with revenue per client
- [ ] Calculate the top 1, top 5, top 10 share
- [ ] Do the same by service line
- [ ] For each concentrated client: contracted or relationship? Ask who holds the relationship.
- [ ] Ask what happens to the business if the largest leaves
- [ ] If the seller can't produce this, note it as a finding rather than a delay

**A concrete threshold, finally named** — the question this page's own FAQ had flagged
as missing: *"if they just have like two accounts making up 50% of revenue, that's kind
of a big deal."* Two clients at half the revenue is offered as the line where
concentration becomes a serious problem worth walking over, not just noting.

## Templates

> "How many clients are there, and do a few of them make up most of the revenue?"
>
> "What share of revenue is your largest client? Your top five?"
>
> "Who holds that relationship — the company, or a person?"

## FAQ

**Q:** What's a safe level of concentration?
**A:** A first threshold now exists: two clients making up 50% of revenue is called out
directly as "a big deal." No fuller scale (e.g. what's fine vs. borderline) has been
given yet — worth Alex naming a complete threshold table.

**Q:** Does this matter if I'm not selling?
**A:** It's the difference between a slow month and an emergency. It matters more when you're
not selling, because there's nobody to catch it.
