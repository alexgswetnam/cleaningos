# Three-Strike System

> [!abstract] In one line
> Log every client complaint against a cleaner as a strike — three, and they're off the
> team — but stay flexible with cleaners who've earned trust over time.

## Definition

A tracking mechanism for cleaner performance: when a client complains about a specific,
substantiated problem tied to a cleaner, it's logged as a strike against that cleaner.
Three strikes and the cleaner is no longer used. Distinct from [[Testing A New Cleaner]]
(a one-time pass/fail evaluation before a cleaner is trusted with real clients) — this is
the *ongoing* accountability system for cleaners already working.

## Why It Matters

At small scale, an owner can track cleaner reliability from memory. That breaks down as
the roster grows — without a written record, decisions about who to keep, who to
reassign, and who to remove become guesswork or gut feel, and patterns that would justify
a decision get lost between one complaint and the next. A logged system also protects
good cleaners from being judged unfairly on a single bad day, since the record shows the
whole pattern, not just the most recent complaint.

## Symptoms

- Cleaner performance issues are tracked in the owner's memory, not written down
- The same type of complaint keeps recurring with the same cleaner without a clear
  decision point
- Uncertainty about whether a given cleaner is actually a repeat problem or just had one
  bad clean
- No consistent standard for when a cleaner gets removed from the roster

## Common Mistakes

- Not logging complaints at all until there's already a crisis
- Applying the three-strike count rigidly to a long-tenured, generally excellent cleaner
  without accounting for their track record
- Treating every complaint as equally serious regardless of whether it was the cleaner's
  fault — see [[Quality Complaints]]'s fault matrix for that upstream decision
- Letting a demonstrated pattern continue because letting the cleaner go feels harsh — see
  [[Hire Slow Fire Fast]]

## Models

**Log complaints, re-cleans, and who did the job — every time, not just when it feels
significant.** The record is what turns "I think this cleaner has problems" into an
actual pattern that can be acted on. This should capture what went wrong, which cleaner
was assigned, and the resolution.

**Three strikes is the stated default, not a rigid rule applied without judgment.** A
long-tenured, well-liked cleaner may accumulate several strikes over many months — sometimes
because of situations that weren't really their fault, or difficult clients rather than
genuine mistakes — without being removed, because the pattern across the full tenure
still supports keeping them. *"None of our cleaners that are with us today have three
strikes — they're out. We're not super hard-ball on that if somebody's been with us for a
while."* The system is a decision aid, not an auto-fire trigger; it still requires
judgment about context.

**Use the data to place, not just to remove.** The same tracking reveals that a given
cleaner is strong at one job type and weak at another — e.g., excellent on recurring
standard cleans but not a fit for deep cleans or move-outs. That's a scheduling/placement
decision, not a strike-worthy failure. See [[Testing A New Cleaner]]'s Student Examples
for the same underlying case (Erica).

**The three-strike record also feeds hiring decisions going forward** — patterns across
cleaners over time (which sourcing channel, which onboarding gaps, which red flags)
surface faster with a written log than from memory.

## Checklist

- [ ] Every substantiated client complaint gets logged against the responsible cleaner
- [ ] Log includes what happened, who was assigned, and the resolution
- [ ] Use [[Quality Complaints]]'s fault matrix first — only log a strike when it's
      genuinely the cleaner's error, not a client asking beyond scope
- [ ] Three strikes is the default trigger for removal — apply judgment for long-tenured,
      high-value cleaners with situational context
- [ ] Review the log periodically to catch patterns (job-type fit, recurring issues) not
      just individual incidents

## Student Examples

**Erica.** One of the company's best, longest-tenured cleaners (~10 months), technically
carrying six or seven strikes — but kept, because her tenure and overall client
satisfaction outweighed the raw count, and several strikes traced to difficult clients
rather than her own errors. Also identified through this same tracking as strong on
recurring standard cleans but not a fit for deep cleans or move-outs, and scheduled
accordingly.

## FAQ

**Q:** Does a strike count if the complaint turns out to be the client's fault, not the
cleaner's?
**A:** No — run it through [[Quality Complaints]]'s fault matrix first. A strike is for a
cleaner's substantiated error, not for every client complaint that comes in.
