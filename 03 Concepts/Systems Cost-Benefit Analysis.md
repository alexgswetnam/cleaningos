# Systems Cost-Benefit Analysis

> [!abstract] In one line
> There's no universal price ceiling for a system — weigh what it costs against what it
> returns (money, time, or reduced burnout), and let that number decide, not a gut feel
> about the sticker price.

## Definition

The recurring decision heuristic Alex names explicitly, by the same phrase, across three
separate decisions: whether to adopt a CRM, whether to hire or extend a VA, and whether to
keep paying for any system generally. Rather than duplicating the same reasoning on each
of those pages, this page holds the heuristic itself; [[Harvest CRM]], [[When To Hire A VA]], and [[Weekend Operations]] link here and apply it to their specific decision.

## Why It Matters

Without an explicit method, system spending gets judged by sticker price alone — "$500 a
month feels like a lot" — which produces two opposite mistakes: paying for tools that
never earn their keep, and refusing tools that would pay for themselves ten times over
because the monthly number looks scary in isolation. A fixed cost ceiling ("never pay more
than $X/month for software") is also wrong for the same reason a fixed VA hourly ceiling
is wrong — it ignores what the spend produces.

## Symptoms

- Judging a subscription or a hire by its price alone, without estimating what it returns
- Keeping a system for months with no clear read on whether it's paying for itself
- Refusing to spend on something that would demonstrably make more than it costs
- Treating "expensive" and "not worth it" as the same judgment

## Common Mistakes

- Only counting revenue as the benefit, and missing time freed up, reduced burnout, or
  better morale as real returns
- Never setting a checkpoint — paying for a system for six or seven months without ever
  asking whether it created any return
- Cancelling something useful because the monthly number feels big, without running the
  actual comparison
- Applying a flat dollar ceiling ("I'll never pay more than $50/month for software")
  instead of comparing cost to what that specific system produces

## Models

**The question, asked the same way every time:** what does this cost, and what does it
create? "Create" is deliberately broader than revenue — it includes freed-up time, reduced
burnout, better morale, and the ability to focus on higher-value work instead of the task
the system now handles.

**Three worked examples, same method, different numbers:**

| Decision | Cost | Return | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| A $500/month marketing system (adjacent-business example, not cleaning-specific) | $500/mo | One additional deal worth $3,300 profit | Worth it — "I'm paying $500 to make $3,300" |
| CRM adoption | ~$200/mo | Even one or two extra bookings a month, plus time freed from manual follow-up | Worth it once lead volume exists to justify it — see [[Harvest CRM]] |
| Weekend/after-hours VA coverage | ~$75/week (3 hrs/day × $5/hr × 5 days) | One extra booking/week at ~$234 average revenue (~$117 after labor) | Worth it — see [[Weekend Operations]] |

**A checkpoint, not just an upfront decision.** The analysis isn't one-and-done at
purchase. If a system has run for six or seven months and produced no traceable revenue or
meaningful non-revenue benefit, that's the signal it isn't working — cancel or replace it,
rather than keep paying out of inertia.

**Non-revenue benefits count, but they should still be named specifically.** "My time is
open" or "I don't have to handle the parts of the business I hate" are legitimate returns
in the same analysis as a dollar figure — the discipline is naming *which* benefit is
being claimed, not defaulting to "it just feels worth it."

**There is no universal price ceiling.** $5,000/month for a system is fine if it returns
more than $5,000/month of value; $50/month is expensive if it returns nothing. The dollar
amount alone answers nothing without the other side of the comparison.

## Checklist

- [ ] Write down the actual monthly (or per-use) cost
- [ ] Estimate the realistic revenue return, not the best-case return
- [ ] Name any non-revenue benefit specifically (time freed, burnout reduced, focus
      gained) rather than treating "it feels worth it" as an answer
- [ ] Compare cost to total return, not cost in isolation
- [ ] Set a checkpoint (a few months out) to actually check whether the return showed up
- [ ] Cancel or replace anything that's run past its checkpoint with no traceable return

## FAQ

**Q:** Isn't this just "it depends"?
**A:** No — it's a specific two-sided comparison (cost vs. named return) with a
checkpoint, not a shrug. "It depends" is the diagnosis; this page is the method for
resolving the dependency.
