# Handling Upset Clients

> [!abstract] In one line
> Get on the same side of the table by default — and if that's not enough, get more upset than they are, on purpose.

## Definition

Two techniques for de-escalating an angry or upset client, plus the recovery script for
winning back a client after a real service failure (a cleaner's professionalism issue, a
damage incident). "Same side of the table" is the default. "The angry boat" is a
deliberate, sparingly-used escalation for clients who won't calm down through validation
alone.

## Why It Matters

How a mistake gets handled often matters more to the client than the mistake itself. Jack
nearly lost a weekly client over a cleaner professionalism issue; the recovery script
below is what won her back. Elijah calmed a client over a $650 damage incident using the
same-side-of-the-table technique on the call itself.

## Symptoms

- A client calls upset and your instinct is to explain or defend
- You've said "it's not that big of a deal" and watched it make things worse
- A minor cleaner mistake turned into a canceled recurring client

## Common Mistakes

- Defending or minimizing instead of validating — the opposite of same-side-of-the-table
- Firing a cleaner over a minor, one-off professionalism issue instead of setting a clear
  standard and moving on
- Not attempting to win a lost client back — the worst they say is no
- Using the angry boat as a default instead of a last resort

## Models

**Same side of the table — the default.** Don't defend, don't minimize, don't talk *at*
the client. Get shoulder-to-shoulder and look at the problem with them, not across from
them:

> "I totally agree with you. This is unacceptable. This should never have happened. You
> shouldn't have to go through this. Thank you for being so kind and patient about this.
> Tell me more."

Never say "it's not that big of a deal" — it puts you on the opposite side of the table
from where you need to be.

**The angry boat — for the genuinely heated ones, used sparingly.** Only one person can be
in the angry boat. If you match or exceed their anger — directed at the problem, never at
them — they often talk themselves down instead of staying escalated:

> "Are you KIDDING me? They did that? I might have to let that cleaner go!"

Counterintuitively, the client often starts defending the person you're both supposedly
angry at: *"No, no, it's not that big a deal, I'm sure it was a mistake."* You've pulled
them out of the angry boat by climbing in yourself. This is a deliberate, occasional tool —
same-side-of-the-table is the default for a reason.

**Winning back a client after a real service failure.** A cleaner's mistake doesn't have
to mean losing the account. Acknowledge, fix the standard, offer a real gesture, ask
directly:

> "The girls love cleaning your home. I understand this was unprofessional. We talked to
> them. Would you be willing to give us another chance? I'd love to offer a discount on
> the next clean as an apology."

**Some clients aren't de-escalation candidates — they're a walk-away decision.** Not every
difficult client is a validation problem. Jack had a client with a documented history of
one-star reviews across small businesses, no red flags on the intake call, who sat in a
camp chair refusing to let cleaners enter without her, made a cleaner cry, had the support
cleaner removed mid-clean, and left a one-star review anyway despite immaculate work. The
coaching response wasn't a better script — it was: you did the best you could, and that
was right; learn from it; then turn it into an SOP, because at scale this repeats. *"The
juice isn't worth the squeeze"* with litigious or aggressive clients: send the cleaners
home, eat the cost, move on, rather than escalating further.

**Write the SOP as generalized guidelines, not scenario-by-scenario rules.** You can't
write "if a cleaner cries, then..." for every possible situation. What transfers to a VA
is a general standard for three recurring situations: a customer being difficult, a
cleaner feeling unsafe, and a cleaner wanting to leave before a job is done. The reason
this matters at all revenue levels: at $50K/month, the situations that felt rare at
$15K/month happen three to four times more often — the owner's gut instinct doesn't scale,
a written standard does. See [[SOPs]].

**Two operational signals worth building into the SOP itself:** low-cost promotional jobs
(e.g. $99 specials) correlate heavily with problem clients — $150+ clients rarely cause
trouble. And a cancellation policy belongs in writing before it's needed: one free
reschedule for recurring clients, a cancellation fee for non-recurring.

**The professionalism issue itself is usually a slap on the wrist, not a firing.** A
cleaner eating lunch at a client's table while the client was out is unprofessional, not
a firing offense. Set a clear standard going forward (don't eat in a client's home —
microwave elsewhere, eat in the car) and move on. Reserve termination for patterns, not
one-off lapses in judgment.

## Checklist

- [ ] Client is upset — default to same-side-of-the-table, not defense
- [ ] Validate first: "I totally agree, this shouldn't have happened"
- [ ] Never say "it's not that big of a deal"
- [ ] If they won't de-escalate through validation, consider the angry boat — deliberately,
      sparingly, aimed at the problem not the person
- [ ] For a real service failure: acknowledge, set the standard with the cleaner, offer a
      genuine gesture, ask for another chance
- [ ] Don't fire a cleaner over a one-off lapse — reserve that for patterns
- [ ] Recognize when a client isn't a de-escalation case — litigious/abusive, walk away
- [ ] Write the difficult-client SOP as general guidelines, not scenario-specific scripts
- [ ] Watch $99-special clients more closely than $150+ clients
- [ ] Put a cancellation policy in writing before it's needed
