# Owner-Dependent Revenue

> [!abstract] In one line
> Revenue that only exists because the owner is working for free. It looks like profit and it isn't.

## Definition

The share of a business's income that depends on the owner personally — his hands, his
relationships, his judgment — rather than on a system that would keep producing without him.

The financial version of the same thing: **unpaid owner labor inflates reported profit.** If
the owner does work he doesn't pay himself for, the cost of that work is missing from the
books, and every profit number downstream is overstated by exactly that much.

## Why It Matters

This is the thing a buyer is really buying, and the thing a seller is really selling. The
vault already says it from the sell side — *"SOPs = replacing yourself... that's what a
buyer is actually purchasing"* ([[SOPs]]). This is what it looks like from the buy side, and
the view is less flattering.

For an owner: owner-dependent revenue is what keeps you at [[Four Roles|Self-Employed]] no
matter what the top line says. A business doing well on paper that stops the week you stop
is not in [[Four Seasons|Harvest]] — it's a job with good margins.

For a buyer: it's the difference between a fair price and a bad one, and it will not be
volunteered.

## Symptoms

**In a business you're buying:**

- The owner describes himself as "managing" a service line, and the hours don't add up
- One person is the only one trusted to do a high-margin task
- The owner's salary line looks small relative to the work he describes
- Revenue is concentrated in the service the owner personally delivers

**In your own business:**

- You couldn't take a month off without the revenue moving
- Clients ask for you by name and won't take a substitute
- You do work you don't pay yourself for, and think of it as saving money

## Common Mistakes

- **Taking SDE at face value.** See [[Add-Backs And SDE]].
- Believing "the owner just handles admin" without asking for the hours
- Not backing out the replacement cost of unpaid labor before valuing anything
- As an owner, treating unpaid labor as thrift. It's a loan to your business at a
  100% discount, and it makes your business look better than it is to you as well as to a
  buyer.

## Models

**The worked example**, from a 25-year-old commercial cleaning business:

| | |
|---|---|
| Employees | ~8, long-tenured |
| Owner's stated role | Management |
| Owner's actual role | Personally does or manages the one-time cleanups |
| That service line | **~25% of revenue** |
| Owner's labor | **15–20 hours/week**, sometimes more |
| Owner's pay for it | **$0** |

His reason: *"no one cleans better than me."*

> "You can't say 128 SDE or whatever when you didn't pay for the labor of 25% of that."

**The correction.** A new owner who won't do the work has to hire someone who will. So:

```
Reported SDE
  − annual cost of replacing the owner's unpaid labor
  = SDE you would actually receive
```

Nothing about the business changed. What changed is that the number is now true.

**Two risks, not one.** The financial one is the obvious half. The other is that a single
person being the only one trusted with 25% of revenue is a concentration risk in its own
right — see [[Client Concentration]]. Even if you paid him fairly, that line dies the day he
leaves unless someone else has been taught.

**It compounds with uncontracted revenue.** If the owner also holds the client
relationships and there are no written contracts, then his departure threatens the revenue
twice over. See [[Uncontracted Recurring Revenue]].

## Checklist

**Buying:**

- [ ] Ask directly how many hours a week the owner works *in* the business, not on it
- [ ] Ask which of those hours he pays himself for
- [ ] Ask which service lines he personally delivers, and what % of revenue they are
- [ ] Ask who else is trained to do them
- [ ] Price the replacement labor at market rate
- [ ] Subtract it from SDE before valuing anything
- [ ] Note that this will not be disclosed unless you ask — it wasn't here

**Selling, or preparing to:**

- [ ] Pay yourself for the labor you perform, on the books
- [ ] Identify every task only you can do
- [ ] Document and hand off one per month — see [[SOPs]]

## Templates

> "How many hours a week is he actually working in the business? And is he paying himself

>
> "Which parts of the work does he personally do? Who else is trained to do them?"

## Student Examples

**The seller in the broker call** — the worked example above. Not a student; a cautionary
counterparty.

**Alex and [[Elijah]]** — the inverse. The reason they could decline a $50K offer and hold
was that *"me holding on... looks like me going, okay, no, we're not going to do anything and
just continue to pay payroll on at the VA and run it."* Almost no owner-dependent revenue,
so waiting was nearly free. See [[Business Valuation]].

## AI Prompts

> When anyone asks whether a business's numbers are good, ask two questions before
> answering: how many hours does the owner work in the business, and does he pay himself for
> them. Until both are answered, the profit figure is unverified regardless of how detailed
> the books look.

## FAQ

**Q:** Isn't every small business owner-dependent to some degree?
**A:** Yes — the question is how much, and whether it's priced in. The failure isn't
dependence, it's dependence nobody quantified.

**Q:** Does this apply to a business I'm not selling?
**A:** Especially then. It's the measurement of whether you own a business or a job. See
[[Four Roles]].
