# Subcontracting Add-On Services

> [!abstract] In one line
> When a client wants something you don't offer, subcontract it and stay the point of contact — or buy the equipment once demand is proven.

## Definition

The decision framework for adding a service outside core house cleaning (carpet cleaning
is the worked example) when a client asks for it: subcontract it out while remaining the
sole point of contact, or invest in owning the capability once demand justifies it.

## Why It Matters

Saying no to an add-on request risks losing the whole relationship, not just the extra
line item — especially with a commercial client who'd rather consolidate vendors than
manage two. Subcontracting lets an owner say yes immediately, with margin, and zero
capital risk; owning the equipment is the next step once the add-on proves itself
repeatedly.

## Symptoms

- A client asks for a service you don't currently offer
- Turning down an add-on request that risks the whole account, not just the add-on
- Already saying yes informally but with no real process or margin captured

## Common Mistakes

- Giving the client the subcontractor's direct contact information — this transfers the
  relationship away from you
- Skipping quotes and going with the first subcontractor found
- Buying equipment before there's repeat demand to justify it
- Not upselling the new capability to other existing clients once you have it

## Models

**Option 1: Subcontract.** Get two to three quotes from local providers of the add-on
service. Walk them through the job and be transparent about the arrangement:

> "I'm looking for a subcontractor I can make a little margin on."

Price the client at the subcontractor's quote plus your margin (e.g. a $200 subcontractor
quote becomes $250–$300 to the client). The critical rule: **you are the point of
contact, always.** The client never receives the subcontractor's information.

> "This is not your client. I am your client."

**Option 2: Buy the equipment.** Once the add-on shows up repeatedly, owning it improves
margins and gives more control. A carpet cleaning machine, for example, runs roughly
$300–$400 to buy versus ~$40 to rent per job. Train an existing cleaner — if another
cleaner's partner or a contact already has the skill, a one-time paid training session is
enough to get a current cleaner up to speed. Once owned, the capability can be upsold
across the rest of the client base, not just the account that originally asked for it.

**Announce the new capability to your database once it's live.** A simple message —
*"We now offer professional carpet cleaning"* — surfaces latent demand from existing
clients who didn't know to ask.

**The add-on can become its own line of business.** One owner in the source runs roughly
$30K/month in carpet cleaning alongside $20K/month in house cleaning — evidence this isn't
just a favor to keep a client happy, it can scale independently.

## Checklist

- [ ] Get 2–3 quotes from local subcontractors for the requested service
- [ ] Be transparent with the subcontractor about the margin arrangement
- [ ] Never give the client the subcontractor's contact info — you stay the point of contact
- [ ] Price at subcontractor cost plus margin
- [ ] Once demand repeats, evaluate buying the equipment and training a current cleaner
- [ ] Announce the new capability to your full database, not just the client who asked

## Templates

- "I'm looking for a subcontractor I can make a little margin on."
- "This is not your client. I am your client."
- "We now offer professional carpet cleaning" — database announcement once capability is live.
