# When To Fire A Client

> [!abstract] In one line
> Ask what that schedule spot would be worth with a better-fit client — if the honest
> answer is "more than this one," offer the current client a price that would actually
> make it worth keeping them, and let them choose.

## Definition

The decision to end an existing client relationship — distinct from
[[Not Every Client Is Your Client]], which is about *not taking on* a low-value client in
the first place. This page is about actively removing one already on the schedule,
because keeping them has an opportunity cost the business is no longer willing to pay.
Named to parallel [[When To Fire A VA]] — the same underlying discipline applied to a
different relationship.

## Why It Matters

A client who's technically paying can still be a net cost to the business through
schedule disruption, low price relative to drive time, late or difficult payment,
frequent rescheduling, or the toll on the cleaner assigned to them. Every recurring spot
on the schedule has an opportunity cost: a better-fit client could occupy that same time,
pay more, create less friction, and make the cleaner's day easier. Keeping a
below-threshold client isn't neutral — it's actively costing the business the client that
spot could otherwise hold.

## Symptoms

- A client requires an inconvenient, fixed time slot that blocks higher-value bookings
- The price for a job no longer reflects the actual drive time, difficulty, or hassle
  involved
- A client pays late, reschedules frequently, or is generally difficult, and the cleaner
  assigned to them dislikes the job
- The owner is keeping a client purely out of habit — they were an early client and it
  feels wrong to let them go

## Common Mistakes

- Keeping a client simply because they're already on the schedule
- Ignoring a pattern of late payments or frequent rescheduling instead of pricing or
  ending the relationship
- Letting a low-paying, inconvenient job block a spot a higher-value client could fill
- Not factoring in cleaner drive time and frustration as a real cost, not just client
  price
- Firing a client emotionally, in the moment, rather than as a calculated business
  decision
- Skipping the step of offering a price that *would* make it worth continuing — jumping
  straight to termination without giving the client the option

## Models

**The core question:** is this schedule spot worth more with a different client? Factor
in price relative to time on-site, drive time for the assigned cleaner, how frequently the
client requires rescheduling or creates friction, payment reliability, and how the
cleaner feels about the account.

**This changes by business stage — deliberately, not as an inconsistency.** A newer,
smaller business will tolerate clients and terms it won't tolerate once it's bigger and
has better options filling the pipeline. There's no fixed threshold; the standard is
meant to tighten over time. See [[Four Seasons]] for the general principle that what's
acceptable shifts by Season.

**Don't jump straight to termination — offer the price that would make it worth
keeping.** Rather than simply dropping a client, calculate what price would actually make
the relationship worth the schedule spot, and offer it honestly:

> "We're not able to continue with our route and our paths and the distance unless it's
> this amount. I totally understand this might be frustrating for you, but it only would
> make sense for us to continue this cleaning if it was [price]."

This respects the client (they get to choose) while protecting the business (the
relationship only continues on terms that actually work). If they decline, the exit is
clean and non-adversarial: *"I apologize. I wish you the best and hope you find a great
cleaner that works out for you."*

**Weigh the full cost, not just the quoted price.** A biweekly client paying $80–90 for a
short, easy clean can still be a net negative if the required time slot blocks a bigger
job, the client is a difficult conversation every visit, and the cleaner's drive is
long. Conversely, a client paying less than ideal but requiring minimal friction and no
schedule conflict may still be worth keeping.

## Checklist

- [ ] Identify exactly what the client is costing: schedule spot, drive time, payment
      friction, cleaner frustration
- [ ] Ask whether a different client would fill that spot better
- [ ] Determine the price or terms that would make the current client worth keeping
- [ ] Offer that price/terms honestly and directly, rather than silently ending the
      relationship
- [ ] If declined, end the relationship professionally and free the spot for a better fit
- [ ] Recognize the threshold tightens as the business grows — don't apply Survival-season
      tolerance indefinitely

## Student Examples

**A biweekly client, ~$80–90/clean.** Required a fixed 1–2pm slot that blocked a larger
job, was a difficult conversation every visit, and had a longer-than-average cleaner
drive. Offered continuation only at $150/clean; outcome not specified in the source, but
the price was named as the actual threshold that would make the spot worth keeping.

**A weekly client, $100/clean, early in the business.** Paid late via Zelle, rescheduled
or skipped weeks frequently, and the assigned cleaner disliked the account. Same
resolution model: continue only at a higher price, or end the relationship.
