# When To Fire A VA

> [!abstract] In one line
> Judge by KPIs, not affection — a VA doesn't have to be better than the owner, but a
> close rate a third of the owner's own is a business decision, not a training problem.

## Definition

The termination decision for a virtual assistant. Distinct from [[Picking The Right VA]]
(who to hire) and [[Managing A VA]] (how to run one day to day) — this page is about
recognizing when the relationship should end and acting on it.

## Why It Matters

Emotional attachment is the default failure mode in a small business — the owner works
closely with one or two people, and a VA who's likeable is easy to keep past the point
the numbers justify it. That delay has a cost: a VA closing at a third of the owner's rate
is capping revenue every week the decision gets deferred.

> "The performance and value of a VA… is really going to be determined by your KPIs."

## Symptoms

- Close rate meaningfully below the owner's own baseline (the named example: owner ~60%,
  VA ~20%)
- Constantly double-checking work that should be trustworthy by now
- A feeling that a week away would let things fall through the cracks
- Not being "stoked" about the VA's performance — a vague but real gut signal
- Explaining away repeated mistakes as one-offs when they're a pattern

## Common Mistakes

- **Keeping a VA because of personal fondness.** The harder the relationship, the harder
  the eventual call — worth naming explicitly rather than pretending affection isn't a
  factor.
- **Not tracking close rate or speed to lead**, so there's no baseline to compare against
  and the decision stays a feeling instead of a number.
- **Treating a pattern of dropped balls as a string of one-off mistakes.** Everyone makes
  mistakes; the question is frequency and whether they're getting worse or staying flat.
- **Waiting for the VA to fail catastrophically before acting**, rather than acting once
  the KPI gap is already clear.
- **Confusing "not as good as me" with "not good enough."** A VA doesn't have to match the
  owner's own performance — see the FAQ.

## Models

**The primary KPI: close rate, when sales are part of the role.** The clearest example on
record: owners at roughly 60%, first VA at roughly 20% — described flatly as "a big
no-no." A same-VA replacement performing near or above the owner's own rate is the
positive comparison case.

**Secondary KPIs, roughly ordered by how easy they are to check:**

- **Speed to lead** — how fast they jump on and call back a new lead
- **Follow-up discipline** — whether everyone who should be contacted actually is
- **Pipeline accuracy** — whether stages and contacts are kept current (the harder one to
  spot-check; requires actually going in and verifying, not just trusting it's happening)
- **Tracking accuracy** — sheets and logs kept correctly
- **Customer service quality** — a proxy the source names directly: how many bad reviews
  trace back to a mishandled upset customer

**The gut check is real data, not noise.** Alongside the KPIs: does the owner feel
confident leaving for a week, or does everything feel like it needs double-checking? That
feeling — described as wanting to feel "stoked" about the VA rather than merely
not-worried — preceded and then confirmed the decision to look for a replacement in the
source example.

**A VA doesn't have to outperform the owner — just be good enough.** "Good enough" is
owner-specific and won't be identical from business to business. The bar isn't parity with
the owner's own skill; it's whether the numbers and the gut both clear a threshold the
owner can live with.

**No baseline to judge against? Get one from someone else before deciding.** If close
rate, speed to lead, and the rest don't mean anything yet because there's no reference
point, that's a case for asking in the community or getting on a call rather than guessing
— not a case for delaying the decision indefinitely.

**Separate the business decision from the personal one, deliberately.** *"Some decisions
consider your heart, but your heart shouldn't guide the decision."* Treating people well
through the termination and making the call on the numbers are not in tension — the
source's account of a VA who was kept on and later helped place with another member's
business models the "still consider people, still make the business call" balance.

## Checklist

- [ ] Establish a close-rate baseline for the role before judging a VA's numbers against
      it
- [ ] Track speed to lead and follow-up discipline, not just close rate
- [ ] Spot-check pipeline and tracking accuracy directly rather than assuming it's correct
- [ ] Watch bad-review rate as a lagging signal on customer-service handling
- [ ] Notice the gut check: would a week away feel safe, or does it feel like things would
      slip?
- [ ] If there's no baseline to compare against, get one (a coaching call, a community
      post) rather than guessing
- [ ] When the decision is made, separate it from the personal relationship — still treat
      the person well, still make the call

## Student Examples

**Alex's first VA (cleaning business)** — closed at roughly 20% against a 60% owner
baseline; things were "slipping through the cracks," and the emotional attachment made
the decision harder than it needed to be. Let go; replaced by the VA still with the
business today, whose close rate now tracks near the owner's own.

**Kyle** — a support VA under the main VA, with a more disconnected owner relationship.
Never actually needed to be fired, but Alex notes the decision would have been easier
precisely because the relationship stayed more businesslike — he was later placed with
another member's business instead.

## FAQ

**Q:** Does the VA need to be as good as me?
**A:** No. "A VA doesn't have to be better than me… but are they doing good enough?" —
good enough is a real, lower bar than parity, and it's set per-owner, not universally.
