# Form Field Optimization

> [!abstract] In one line
> Every extra form field loses submissions — qualify on the phone, not the form.

## Definition

The documented marketing relationship between quote-form length and submission rate:
more fields consistently means fewer completed submissions, regardless of industry.
Applied to a cleaning business's website quote form specifically.

## Why It Matters

A long form feels like it produces "better" leads by pre-qualifying them, but it does so
by losing people at the form itself — including people who would have become good
clients if the friction weren't there. The trade is invisible because the lost visitors
never show up anywhere to be counted.

## Symptoms

- A quote form with many fields (address details, service specifics, timing preferences,
  etc.) beyond the basics
- Low form-completion rate relative to site traffic
- A belief that a longer form produces "better" leads without evidence it's a net gain

## Common Mistakes

- Adding fields to pre-qualify leads without weighing the submissions lost to friction
- Treating a long form as free quality control instead of a real cost
- Not testing the trade-off — assuming rather than measuring

## Models

**The law, stated plainly:**

> "Increased form fields equal decreased number of form submissions... you're missing the
> people that would have been a client but aren't because a quick dopamine hit, brain
> friction thing caused them to stop."

**The minimal form.** Alex's own form: name, phone, and an optional message field —
nothing else. The VA calls back within seconds. Qualification happens on the call, not on
the form. `Cleaning Biz 101 — Acquire` states the identical field set independently (name,
phone, optional note) with the same reasoning made explicit: *"the goal of the website is
not to perfectly qualify them!!! The goal is to capture the lead and then let your
follow-up + phone call do the real work."*

**The counterargument, and why it's outweighed.** A deeper form does pre-qualify —
someone willing to fill out five fields is more serious than someone who bounces after
two. But the people lost to friction outnumber the bad leads filtered out, and there's a
second-order problem: price shoppers willing to fill out a long form just to compare
quotes can close for the wrong reason and become bad clients anyway.

> "It's better to have no client than to have a bad client."

**Test it, don't assume it.** Take the long form down for a week, replace it with the
minimal version, and watch whether more people reach out. Pair with [[Conversion
Tracking|Microsoft Clarity]] to see exactly where visitors were dropping off on the old
form.

## Checklist

- [ ] Reduce the quote form to name, phone, and an optional message
- [ ] Move qualification to the phone call, not the form
- [ ] A/B test: swap the long form for the short one for a week and compare submissions
- [ ] Watch Clarity session recordings for drop-off points on the current form

**A:** Partially, but the source's read is that the volume lost to friction outweighs the
filtering benefit, and long-form price shoppers can still turn into bad clients anyway —
the filtering isn't as effective as it feels.
