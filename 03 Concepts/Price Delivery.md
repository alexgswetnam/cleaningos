# Price Delivery

> [!abstract] In one line
> The price is a statement, not a question. Say it with a period at the end.

## Definition

How the number sounds when it leaves your mouth. Same price, same words — the inflection
decides whether the prospect hears a quote or an apology.

## Why It Matters

> "I have my quote here at 180 plus tax... is that something you're comfortable moving
> forward with?"

Read that out loud and hear where your voice goes at the end. Up. It's a question, and the
question is *please don't be upset with me.* The prospect hears someone who doesn't believe
the price, and concludes — reasonably — that the price is negotiable.

> "So it's just going to be 180 plus tax. Go ahead and grab your card when you're ready."

Down. Same number. Nothing to respond to.

This is the single most common fixable defect in a sales call, and it costs nothing to fix.

## Symptoms

- Your voice rises at the end of the number
- You follow the price with a permission question
- You add justification nobody asked for
- You feel a flicker of apology before you say it

## Common Mistakes

- **Upward inflection.** Turns a price into a request.
- **Following the price with "is that something you're comfortable with?"** — you've handed
  them the decision to reject it.
- Explaining the price before anyone objects
- Rushing past the number as though hoping it won't be noticed
- Fixing the tone while still not believing the price — see below; technique alone doesn't hold

## Models

**Three tonalities, from Chris Voss**, each with a specific job:

| Tonality | Sounds like | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| **Late night DJ** — slow, steady, deep | *"We cannot get on ladders. At all."* | Non-negotiables. There's no room, and the voice says so before the words do. |
| **Heavy** — pause, weight, space | *"This is going to... change your life."* | The one thing you want to land |
| **Light** — casual, quick, the word *just* | *"It's just going to be five thousand dollars. Want to go ahead and get started?"* | Prices. Especially big ones. |

**"Just" is the whole trick.** It's a minimiser, and it works on any number. *It's just going
to be $180.* *It's just five thousand dollars.* The word tells the listener how big a deal
this is supposed to be, and they mostly agree with you.

**Practice, because this is physical.** Record yourself. Stand in front of a mirror. Say the
price out loud until it stops sounding like a question.

> It will feel dumb — do it anyway.

**Technique is the smaller half.** Tonality masks insecurity for a while; it doesn't remove
it.

> "If you feel like you're overcharging, your prospect will feel it."

The fix is market research: call competitors, compare, confirm your price is fair. Genuine
belief that your price is a good deal produces the right voice without any technique at all.
Note the direction of causation — the confidence isn't self-talk, it's evidence. That's
[[Five Laws|Stop Guessing]] applied to your own pricing.

## Checklist

- [ ] Say the price out loud, recorded, ten times
- [ ] Listen back — does your voice go up or down at the end?
- [ ] Cut any permission question that follows the number
- [ ] Add "just"
- [ ] Follow the price immediately with the assumed close
- [ ] Separately: call three competitors and confirm your price is fair

## FAQ

**Q:** What if they *do* object after a confident delivery?
**A:** Then it's a real objection rather than one you invited. See [[Price Objection]].

**Q:** Doesn't "just" sound like I'm minimising their money?
**A:** It minimises the *decision*, not the money. Said quickly and casually it reads as "this
is routine," which is what you want it to be.
